Retinoblastoma is a malignant tumor of the retina, usually in young children.

Break the word apart:

* retino- = retina
* blast = immature precursor cell
* -oma = tumor

So retinoblastoma is basically:

> cancer from immature retinal cells.

---

### Core idea

The retina normally has immature cells that should stop dividing and mature into light-sensing cells.

In retinoblastoma, the **RB1 tumor suppressor gene** is lost.

RB1 normally acts like a brake on the cell cycle.

When both RB1 copies are knocked out:

* cells lose the G1/S checkpoint
* E2F transcription factor is overactive
* retinal precursor cells keep dividing
* tumor forms in the retina

G1/S checkpoint means the point where a cell decides whether to enter DNA synthesis phase.

---

### Classic presentation

Most classic finding:

* leukocoria

Leukocoria means white pupil reflex.

Normally, flash photography gives a red reflex because light reflects off the vascular retina.

In retinoblastoma, the tumor blocks/reflects that light, so the pupil can look white.

Other findings:

* strabismus, meaning eyes are misaligned
* vision loss
* painful red eye if advanced
* calcifications inside the eye

---

### Hereditary vs sporadic

Hereditary retinoblastoma:

* germline RB1 mutation, meaning the child is born with one bad copy in every cell
* only needs one more hit in a retinal cell
* often bilateral, meaning both eyes
* often multifocal, meaning multiple tumors
* earlier onset
* increased risk of osteosarcoma later

Sporadic retinoblastoma:

* both RB1 hits happen only in one retinal cell
* usually unilateral, meaning one eye
* usually later than hereditary cases

---

## One-line intuition

Retinoblastoma is what happens when immature retinal cells lose the RB1 “cell-cycle brake,” so they keep dividing and create a white-reflex eye tumor in a child.

---

## Pearl 🔥

RB1 is on chromosome 13q14, and retinoblastoma follows the two-hit hypothesis: both copies of a tumor suppressor gene must be lost for cancer to develop.
