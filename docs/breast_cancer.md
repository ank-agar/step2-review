### Core idea

Breast cancer is malignant growth of breast cells, most commonly from:

* ducts = tubes that carry milk to the nipple
* lobules = glands that make milk

So the big picture is:

> abnormal duct/lobule cells grow too much, invade nearby tissue, and can spread through lymph or blood.

---

### Key types

* **DCIS (ductal carcinoma in situ):** abnormal duct cells are still trapped inside the duct
* **IDC (invasive ductal carcinoma):** duct cancer breaks through the duct wall and invades breast tissue
* **ILC (invasive lobular carcinoma):** cancer starts in lobules and invades surrounding tissue

Break down **in situ**:

* in situ = "in its original place"

So DCIS is like:

> bad-looking cells, but still boxed inside the duct.

Invasive cancer is worse because it has escaped the normal boundary.

---

### Why lymph nodes matter

Breast tissue drains to lymph nodes (immune-system filters), especially the axillary lymph nodes (armpit lymph nodes).

So if cancer cells enter lymph vessels, they often show up first in axillary nodes.

That is why breast cancer staging cares a lot about lymph node involvement.

---

### Important receptor markers

Breast cancers are tested for:

* **ER (estrogen receptor):** tumor grows in response to estrogen
* **PR (progesterone receptor):** tumor grows in response to progesterone
* **HER2 (human epidermal growth factor receptor 2):** growth-signal receptor that can make tumors more aggressive

These matter because they guide treatment.

Example:

* ER-positive tumors can respond to anti-estrogen therapy
* HER2-positive tumors can respond to HER2-targeted therapy
* triple-negative = ER-negative, PR-negative, and HER2-negative

---

### One-line intuition

Breast cancer is usually duct or lobule cells losing growth control, then either staying contained or breaking out and spreading.

---

### HY Step 1 pearl

Most breast cancers are invasive ductal carcinoma, and breast lymph drains mainly to axillary lymph nodes.
