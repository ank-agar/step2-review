## Chondromalacia

### Core idea

Chondromalacia means **softening/damage of cartilage**.

Break it down:

* chondro- = cartilage
* -malacia = softening

Most commonly, people mean **chondromalacia patellae**, which is softening or breakdown of the cartilage under the patella (kneecap).

---

### What is actually happening?

The patella normally glides smoothly over the femur (thigh bone).

In chondromalacia:

* the cartilage under the patella gets softened, rough, or fissured
* the patella no longer glides smoothly
* this causes anterior knee pain, meaning pain in the front of the knee

So the problem is basically:

> damaged cartilage behind the kneecap rubbing during knee movement.

---

### Why the symptoms happen

Pain is usually worse with:

* stairs
* squatting
* kneeling
* running
* sitting for a long time with the knee bent

Reason:

These positions increase pressure between the patella and femur.

More pressure on rough cartilage = more irritation and pain.

The classic "movie theater sign" is pain after sitting for a long time with knees bent.

---

### Common causes

Chondromalacia can happen from:

* overuse
* trauma
* patellar maltracking, meaning the kneecap does not glide in the ideal path
* weak quadriceps, especially the muscles that help stabilize the patella
* abnormal knee alignment

The big intuition:

> bad tracking + repeated load = cartilage wear behind the kneecap.

---

### Diagnosis/treatment idea

Diagnosis is usually clinical, meaning based on history and exam.

MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) can show cartilage damage if needed.

Treatment is usually conservative:

* activity modification
* physical therapy
* strengthening quadriceps/hip muscles
* NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), like ibuprofen, for pain/inflammation

---

## One-line intuition

Chondromalacia is soft, rough cartilage behind the kneecap causing front knee pain when the patella is compressed against the femur.

---

## Pearl

Chondromalacia patellae is a common cause of **anterior knee pain**, especially pain worse with stairs, squatting, or prolonged sitting.
