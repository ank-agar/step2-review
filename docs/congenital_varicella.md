### Core idea

Congenital varicella means fetal infection with varicella-zoster virus (VZV, the chickenpox/shingles virus) during pregnancy.

If mom gets primary varicella, meaning first-time chickenpox infection, early in pregnancy, the virus can cross the placenta and damage developing fetal tissues.

Classic findings:

* limb hypoplasia (underdeveloped limb)
* cicatricial skin scars (scar-like, often in a dermatomal pattern)
* eye problems, like cataracts or chorioretinitis (inflammation/scarring of the retina/choroid)
* neurologic problems, like cortical atrophy (loss of brain tissue), seizures, or developmental delay

---

### Why the findings happen

VZV is neurotropic, meaning it likes nerves.

So in the fetus, it can damage:

* nerves supplying the skin/limbs
* skin over those nerve distributions
* developing brain and eyes

That is why the scars can look dermatomal (following a nerve territory), and why limb defects often occur on the same affected side.

---

### Important timing

Highest-yield risk window:

* maternal chickenpox in the first 20 weeks of pregnancy

Near delivery is different:

* infection around birth can cause neonatal varicella (newborn chickenpox), which is severe disseminated infection, not the same as congenital malformation syndrome.

---

## One-line intuition

Congenital varicella is chickenpox crossing the placenta early and damaging fetal nerves, skin, limbs, eyes, and brain while they are forming.

---

## Pearl

Maternal shingles, also called herpes zoster (reactivation of old VZV), usually does **not** cause congenital varicella because mom already has anti-VZV IgG antibodies (protective immunoglobulin G antibodies) that cross the placenta.
