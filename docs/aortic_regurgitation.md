## Aortic regurgitation

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/74/Blausen_0039_AorticRegurgitation.png)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e5/Dilated_aortic_root_and_aortic_regurgitation_E00237_%28CardioNetworks_ECHOpedia%29.jpg)

Aortic regurgitation means the aortic valve does not close tightly, so blood leaks backward from the aorta into the left ventricle during diastole (heart relaxation/filling).

Normally:

* systole (contraction): left ventricle pushes blood through the aortic valve into the aorta
* diastole: aortic valve closes so blood cannot fall back into the left ventricle

In aortic regurgitation:

* the valve is leaky
* blood goes forward into the aorta, then partly leaks backward into the left ventricle
* the left ventricle gets volume overload

---

### Why the findings happen

The left ventricle receives extra blood every beat:

* normal blood from the left atrium
* regurgitated blood from the aorta

So the left ventricle dilates because it is handling too much volume.

That gives:

* wide pulse pressure = high systolic pressure + low diastolic pressure
* bounding pulses because stroke volume is high
* early diastolic murmur because the leaking happens right after the aortic valve should have closed

Diastolic pressure drops because blood is running backward out of the aorta into the left ventricle during diastole.

---

### Murmur

Classic murmur:

* early diastolic
* decrescendo (gets softer over time)
* best heard along the left sternal border

Think:

> aortic valve leaks during diastole, so the murmur is diastolic.

---

### Common causes

High-yield causes include:

* aortic root dilation
* bicuspid aortic valve
* infective endocarditis
* rheumatic heart disease
* syphilitic aortitis
* aortic dissection

---

## One-line intuition

Aortic regurgitation is a leaky aortic valve, so blood falls back into the left ventricle during diastole and causes left ventricular volume overload.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Aortic regurgitation causes a wide pulse pressure because systolic pressure rises from increased stroke volume, while diastolic pressure falls from blood leaking back into the left ventricle.
