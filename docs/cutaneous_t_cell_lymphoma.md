### Core idea

Cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL) is a **T-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma** that mainly lives in the **skin**.

Break it down:

* cutaneous = skin
* T-cell = immune lymphocyte cell type
* lymphoma = cancer of lymphocytes

So CTCL basically means:

> cancerous T cells collecting in the skin.

---

### Most important type

The classic CTCL is **mycosis fungoides**.

Important: despite the name, mycosis fungoides is **not a fungal infection**.

It is a lymphoma that often progresses slowly:

* patch stage: flat, scaly rash
* plaque stage: thicker raised lesions
* tumor stage: nodular skin tumors

---

### Why the rash happens

The malignant T cells are “skin-homing.”

They move into the epidermis (outer skin layer) and dermis (deeper skin layer), causing:

* chronic scaly patches
* plaques
* itching
* sometimes tumors

Reasoning:

Normal T cells can patrol skin, but in CTCL a clone of abnormal T cells expands and keeps accumulating there.

---

### Histology clue

Classic biopsy finding:

* **Pautrier microabscesses**

These are small clusters of malignant T cells inside the epidermis.

They are called “microabscesses,” but they are **not true pus abscesses**. They are collections of atypical lymphocytes (abnormal immune cells).

---

### Sézary syndrome

Sézary syndrome is the leukemic form of CTCL.

Leukemic means the malignant cells are significantly present in the blood.

Classic findings:

* diffuse red itchy skin = erythroderma
* lymphadenopathy (swollen lymph nodes)
* Sézary cells in blood

Sézary cells are malignant T cells with cerebriform nuclei, meaning folded “brain-like” nuclei.

---

## One-line intuition

CTCL is like a lymphoma that prefers the skin, so malignant T cells pile up there and create chronic eczema-like patches, plaques, or tumors.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Mycosis fungoides can mimic eczema or psoriasis for years, but biopsy shows epidermotropic malignant T cells and sometimes Pautrier microabscesses.
