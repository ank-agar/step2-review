### Core idea

Lipoprotein lipase deficiency is an autosomal recessive enzyme deficiency where **LPL (lipoprotein lipase)** does not work.

Normally, LPL sits on capillary endothelium and breaks down triglycerides inside:

* chylomicrons
* VLDL (very-low-density lipoprotein)

into free fatty acids that tissues can use or store.

So if LPL is deficient:

> chylomicrons cannot be cleared from blood.

Result:

* massively increased triglycerides
* increased chylomicrons
* creamy/milky plasma

---

### Why the findings happen

* **Pancreatitis:** very high triglycerides irritate/injure the pancreas
* **Eruptive xanthomas:** triglyceride-rich lipid deposits in skin
* **Hepatosplenomegaly:** liver and spleen macrophages take up excess lipid
* **Lipemia retinalis:** retinal blood vessels look pale/creamy from fat-filled blood
* **No major premature atherosclerosis:** chylomicrons are huge and do not easily enter vessel walls

---

### Key association

This is **Type I hyperlipoproteinemia**.

Type I = problem clearing chylomicrons.

Most common causes:

* LPL deficiency
* ApoC-II deficiency

ApoC-II (apolipoprotein C-II) activates LPL, so ApoC-II deficiency looks similar because LPL is present but cannot be turned on.

---

## One-line intuition

LPL is the “fat unload enzyme,” so without it, chylomicrons stay stuck in the blood and triglycerides become extremely high.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Lipoprotein lipase is stimulated by insulin, which is why insulin helps move fatty acids into adipose tissue after eating.
