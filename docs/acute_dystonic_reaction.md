### Core idea

An **acute dystonic reaction** is a sudden involuntary muscle spasm/posture caused by a medication.

Most classically from **dopamine D2 receptor blockers**:

* antipsychotics, especially high-potency ones like haloperidol
* antiemetics (anti-nausea meds) like metoclopramide or prochlorperazine

---

### Why it happens

Movement is coordinated in the **basal ganglia** (deep brain motor-control circuits).

Normally:

* dopamine helps balance movement
* acetylcholine also affects movement

If you block dopamine D2 receptors:

* dopamine activity goes down
* acetylcholine activity becomes relatively too strong
* muscles contract abnormally

So the intuition is:

> dopamine blockade removes the "smooth movement" balance, so muscles can lock into painful spasms.

---

### What it can look like

Common dystonic patterns:

* **oculogyric crisis** = eyes forcibly deviate upward
* **torticollis** = neck twists to one side
* jaw spasm/trismus (difficulty opening the mouth)
* tongue protrusion
* back arching
* laryngeal dystonia (voice box spasm), which can threaten the airway

Usually happens within hours to a few days after starting or increasing the offending drug.

---

### Treatment

Give an anticholinergic medication:

* benztropine
* diphenhydramine

Why this works:

* the problem is relative acetylcholine excess
* anticholinergics block acetylcholine
* this restores the dopamine-acetylcholine balance

---

## One-line intuition

Acute dystonic reaction = dopamine blocker causes relative acetylcholine excess in the basal ganglia, making muscles suddenly spasm into abnormal postures.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Acute dystonia is an **extrapyramidal symptom** (EPS; medication-induced movement side effect) that responds fast to **benztropine or diphenhydramine**, while **tardive dyskinesia** (late-onset repetitive movements) is treated differently and can worsen with anticholinergics.
