### Core idea

Drug-induced peripheral neuropathy means nerve damage caused by a medication.

Peripheral nerves = nerves outside the brain and spinal cord.

Neuropathy = nerve dysfunction/damage.

So the idea is:

> a drug injures peripheral nerves, causing abnormal sensation, pain, weakness, or autonomic symptoms.

---

### Classic symptoms

Usually starts in a stocking-glove pattern:

* toes/feet first
* fingers/hands later

Symptoms:

* numbness
* tingling
* burning pain
* pins-and-needles sensation
* decreased vibration/position sense
* weakness sometimes

Stocking-glove pattern happens because the longest nerves are most vulnerable.

---

### Why drugs cause it

Different drugs injure nerves in different ways:

* direct axon damage
* mitochondrial toxicity
* impaired microtubules
* impaired axonal transport
* demyelination sometimes

Axonal transport = the nerve's internal shipping system that moves materials up and down the axon.

Intuition:

The longest nerves need the most maintenance and transport, so when a toxic drug disrupts nerve metabolism/transport, the farthest parts of the nerve die first.

That is why symptoms usually begin in the feet.

---

### High-yield drug causes

Think:

* chemotherapy drugs: vincristine, paclitaxel, cisplatin, oxaliplatin, bortezomib
* isoniazid
* metronidazole
* amiodarone
* linezolid
* nitrofurantoin
* dapsone
* some antiretrovirals

Isoniazid is especially Step 1-relevant because it can cause vitamin B6 deficiency.

Vitamin B6 = pyridoxine, needed for normal nerve function.

---

### One-line intuition

Drug-induced peripheral neuropathy is when a neurotoxic medication damages long peripheral nerves first, so the patient gets symmetric burning/numbness in feet and hands.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Isoniazid can cause peripheral neuropathy by depleting vitamin B6 (pyridoxine), so give pyridoxine with isoniazid to prevent neuropathy.
