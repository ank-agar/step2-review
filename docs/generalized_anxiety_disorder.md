## Generalized anxiety disorder

![Image](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/sites/default/files/images/outreach/social-media-graphics/2021_ImSoStressed_Infographic.jpg)

![Image](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/images/ency/fullsize/17191.jpg)

Generalized anxiety disorder, or GAD (generalized anxiety disorder), is chronic excessive worry about many different things, usually for **6 months or longer**.

The key idea:

* worry is hard to control
* worry is about multiple areas of life
* symptoms interfere with daily functioning

Common worries:

* school/work
* health
* money
* family
* future events

---

### Why the symptoms happen

Think of GAD as the brain’s threat system being set too sensitive.

The amygdala (fear-processing part of the limbic system) acts like an alarm system.

In GAD, that alarm keeps firing even when there is no clear danger.

So the body stays in a mild fight-or-flight state:

* muscle tension
* restlessness
* fatigue
* poor sleep
* difficulty concentrating
* irritability

Why fatigue?

Because constantly scanning for danger uses mental energy, and sympathetic activation (fight-or-flight nervous system activity) keeps the body tense.

---

### Diagnosis intuition

Normal anxiety:

* tied to a real stressor
* improves when the stressor passes

GAD:

* persistent
* excessive
* generalized across many topics
* hard to shut off

One-line intuition:

> GAD is not one fear; it is the brain treating everyday uncertainty like a threat.

---

### Treatment

First-line:

* CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy)
* SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor)
* SNRI (serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor)

CBT helps because it trains the patient to identify and challenge catastrophic thoughts.

SSRIs/SNRIs help because serotonin and norepinephrine signaling modulate mood, fear circuits, and baseline anxiety.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Buspirone is used for GAD and is a **5-HT1A partial agonist** (serotonin 1A receptor partial activator).

HY contrast:

* buspirone: delayed onset, no major sedation, low dependence risk
* benzodiazepines: fast onset, sedating, dependence risk
