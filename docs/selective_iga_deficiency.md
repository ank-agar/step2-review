### Core idea

Selective IgA deficiency is low/absent IgA (immunoglobulin A) with normal IgG (immunoglobulin G) and IgM (immunoglobulin M).

IgA normally protects mucosal surfaces:

* respiratory tract
* GI (gastrointestinal) tract
* saliva
* tears
* breast milk

So if IgA is missing, the "front door" mucosal defense is weak.

---

### Why the findings happen

* Recurrent sinus/lung infections: less IgA on respiratory mucosa, so bacteria attach more easily
* Recurrent diarrhea/Giardia: less IgA in the gut, so intestinal pathogens are harder to block
* Allergies/asthma/atopy: mucosal immune regulation is abnormal
* Autoimmune disease risk: immune tolerance at mucosal surfaces is disrupted

Atopy means allergic-type disease, like eczema, allergic rhinitis, or asthma.

---

### Why it is called "selective"

It is selective because mainly IgA is low.

Other antibodies are usually normal:

* IgG normal
* IgM normal

So this is not a total B-cell problem.

It is more like:

> B cells exist, but they do not mature well into IgA-producing plasma cells.

---

### Transfusion reaction

Some patients can make anti-IgA antibodies.

If they receive blood products containing IgA, those anti-IgA antibodies can trigger anaphylaxis.

Anaphylaxis means severe immediate allergic reaction with hypotension, wheezing, swelling, or shock.

---

## One-line intuition

IgA guards mucosal surfaces, so IgA deficiency mainly causes respiratory/GI infections, plus allergy/autoimmunity risk.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Selective IgA deficiency can cause a false-negative celiac screen if the test uses IgA antibodies, like anti-tTG IgA (anti-tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin A).
