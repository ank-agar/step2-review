Central retinal artery occlusion, or CRAO (central retinal artery occlusion), is basically:

* a blockage of the main artery supplying the inner retina
* causing sudden, painless vision loss in one eye

Think of it like a stroke of the retina.

---

### Core idea

The central retinal artery brings blood to the inner retina.

If it gets blocked:

* inner retina becomes ischemic (low oxygen from poor blood flow)
* retina swells and turns pale/white
* vision suddenly drops

The classic funduscopy (looking at the retina with an ophthalmoscope) finding is:

* pale retina
* cherry-red spot at the macula

---

### Why the cherry-red spot happens

The surrounding retina turns pale because it loses blood supply and becomes edematous (swollen with fluid).

But the fovea (center of sharp vision in the macula) is thin and is supplied mostly by the choroid (vascular layer behind the retina), not the central retinal artery.

So the red choroid still shows through at the fovea.

That gives:

> pale ischemic retina around a red fovea = cherry-red spot

---

### Common causes

Usually due to an embolus (traveling clot/debris blocking a vessel), often from:

* carotid atherosclerosis (plaque buildup in the carotid artery)
* cardiac emboli (clots/debris from the heart)

In an older patient, also think about giant cell arteritis (large-vessel vasculitis causing headache, jaw claudication, and vision loss).

---

## One-line intuition

CRAO is when the retina suddenly loses its arterial blood supply, so the retina becomes pale and blind, while the thin fovea still looks red.

---

## HY pearl

Sudden painless monocular vision loss (vision loss in one eye) + cherry-red spot = central retinal artery occlusion until proven otherwise.

Also, CRAO is an emergency because it can signal systemic embolic disease or giant cell arteritis.
