### Core idea

Antithrombin III deficiency is an inherited thrombophilia (tendency to form clots) where you do not have enough functional antithrombin III.

Antithrombin III is a natural anticoagulant (clot-preventing protein).

It normally inhibits:

* thrombin, also called factor IIa (activated factor 2)
* factor Xa (activated factor 10)

So if antithrombin III is low:

* thrombin activity goes up
* factor Xa activity goes up
* clot formation goes up

---

### Why this causes clots

Think:

> Antithrombin III is the brake on thrombin and factor Xa.

If the brake is weak, the coagulation cascade (clotting pathway) keeps pushing toward fibrin clot formation.

That causes venous thrombosis (clots in veins), especially:

* DVT (deep vein thrombosis; clot in a deep vein, usually leg)
* PE (pulmonary embolism; clot travels to lung arteries)

---

### Important heparin connection

Heparin works by activating antithrombin III.

So:

* normal antithrombin III + heparin = strong anticoagulation
* low antithrombin III + heparin = weaker response

This is why antithrombin III deficiency can cause heparin resistance (needing unusually high heparin doses because heparin has less antithrombin III to activate).

---

### Inheritance

Usually autosomal dominant (one mutated copy can cause disease).

The gene is SERPINC1 (gene encoding antithrombin).

---

## One-line intuition

Antithrombin III deficiency removes a natural “clotting brake,” so thrombin and factor Xa stay too active and the patient becomes hypercoagulable (too prone to clotting).

---

## Pearl 🔥

Antithrombin III deficiency causes recurrent venous clots and can make heparin less effective because heparin needs antithrombin III to work.
