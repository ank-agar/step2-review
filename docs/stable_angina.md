### Core idea

Stable angina is predictable chest pain from a fixed narrowing of a coronary artery (artery that supplies the heart muscle).

Usually caused by atherosclerosis (cholesterol plaque buildup in the artery wall).

The key pattern:

* happens with exertion or stress
* improves with rest
* improves with nitroglycerin (vasodilator drug that lowers heart workload)

---

### Why exertion causes pain

At rest, the narrowed artery can still deliver enough oxygen.

During exercise/stress:

* heart rate goes up
* contractility (squeeze strength) goes up
* myocardial oxygen demand (heart muscle oxygen need) goes up

But the coronary artery is already narrowed, so blood flow cannot increase enough.

So stable angina is mainly:

> demand ischemia = the heart wants more oxygen than the narrowed vessel can supply.

Ischemia (low blood flow/oxygen) causes chest pressure, tightness, or pain.

---

### Why rest helps

Rest lowers myocardial oxygen demand (heart muscle oxygen need).

So now the fixed narrow artery can again supply enough oxygen, and the pain goes away.

---

### Why nitroglycerin helps

Nitroglycerin dilates veins more than arteries.

That lowers preload (blood returning to the heart), so the heart has less volume to pump.

Less workload = less oxygen demand = less angina.

---

### Stable vs unstable

Stable angina:

* fixed plaque
* predictable exertional pain
* relieved by rest/nitro
* no myocardial infarction (heart attack/cell death)
* troponin (heart muscle injury marker) is negative

Unstable angina:

* plaque rupture with thrombus (blood clot)
* pain can happen at rest
* more dangerous
* troponin is still negative

Myocardial infarction (MI; heart attack):

* ischemia severe enough to kill heart cells
* troponin is positive

---

## One-line intuition

Stable angina is a narrowed coronary pipe that works at rest, but cannot increase flow when the heart works harder.

---

## HY Step 1 pearl

Stable angina classically causes transient ST depression on ECG (electrocardiogram) during exercise because it produces subendocardial ischemia (low oxygen in the inner heart wall), not full-thickness infarction.
