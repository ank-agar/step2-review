# Nausea

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK594994/bin/ch7gastro-Image022.jpg)

![Image](https://s3.amazonaws.com/static.wd7.us/e/e0/Vomiting_physiology.jpg)

Nausea = unpleasant feeling that you might vomit.

It is a symptom, not a diagnosis.

Important distinction:

* nausea = feeling like you may throw up
* vomiting, or emesis = actual forceful expulsion of stomach contents
* retching = dry heaving without much coming out

---

### Core idea

Nausea is mainly a brainstem warning signal.

The body senses something potentially bad, then sends signals to the vomiting pathway:

* GI (gastrointestinal) tract irritation, like food poisoning
* vestibular system input, meaning inner ear balance signals, like motion sickness
* blood-borne toxins/drugs
* cortex/limbic system, meaning higher brain/emotion areas, like anxiety, smells, pain

These inputs converge in the medulla (brainstem region that controls autonomic reflexes).

---

### Why nausea feels so bad

The brainstem activates autonomic responses.

Autonomic means automatic body functions you do not consciously control.

That causes:

* increased salivation
* sweating
* pallor (pale skin)
* stomach slowing
* feeling like food is sitting in the stomach
* urge to vomit

So nausea is not just a "stomach feeling."

It is a brainstem + autonomic reflex state.

---

### Area postrema

The area postrema is part of the medulla that detects toxins/drugs in blood.

It acts like the CTZ (chemoreceptor trigger zone), meaning a chemical-sensing region that can trigger nausea/vomiting.

Key idea:

* most brain areas are protected by the BBB (blood-brain barrier)
* area postrema has a weak BBB
* so it can sample blood for toxins

That is why things like chemotherapy, opioids, uremia, and some toxins can cause nausea.

---

### Receptor logic

High-yield nausea/vomiting receptors:

* 5-HT3 (serotonin type 3 receptor): GI tract + CTZ
* D2 (dopamine type 2 receptor): CTZ
* H1 (histamine type 1 receptor): vestibular system
* M1 (muscarinic type 1 acetylcholine receptor): vestibular system
* NK1 (neurokinin 1 receptor): substance P pathway

This is why different antiemetics (anti-vomiting drugs) work in different settings.

Examples:

* ondansetron blocks 5-HT3
* metoclopramide blocks D2
* scopolamine blocks muscarinic receptors
* antihistamines help motion sickness by blocking H1

---

## One-line intuition

Nausea is the brainstem saying, "Something may be toxic or mismatched, prepare to vomit."

---

## Pearl 🔥

Motion sickness nausea is mainly vestibular, so H1 blockers and antimuscarinics help.

Chemotherapy nausea is classically treated with 5-HT3 blockers like ondansetron, often plus NK1 blockers for highly emetogenic (vomit-triggering) regimens.
