### Core idea

Enteroviruses are small, non-enveloped, positive-sense single-stranded RNA viruses.

Positive-sense RNA means the viral RNA can act like mRNA (messenger RNA) and be translated directly by host ribosomes.

They are called enteroviruses because they replicate in the GI tract (gastrointestinal tract), but they can spread to other places like:

* skin
* meninges (brain/spinal cord coverings)
* muscle
* heart
* anterior horn cells (lower motor neuron cell bodies in the spinal cord)

---

### Big examples

Important enteroviruses include:

* poliovirus
* coxsackievirus A
* coxsackievirus B
* echovirus
* enterovirus A71

The common Step 1 pattern is:

* coxsackie A = hand-foot-mouth disease and herpangina
* coxsackie B = myocarditis, pericarditis, pleurodynia
* poliovirus = flaccid paralysis
* echovirus = aseptic meningitis

Aseptic meningitis means meningitis symptoms, but routine bacterial culture is negative.

---

### Why the findings happen

Enteroviruses usually spread fecal-orally.

So the pathway is:

* virus enters mouth
* replicates in oropharynx/GI tract
* spreads through blood
* seeds target tissues

That is why one virus family can cause different syndromes:

* skin/mucosa infection → vesicles in hand-foot-mouth disease
* meningeal infection → headache, neck stiffness, aseptic meningitis
* heart infection → myocarditis/pericarditis
* anterior horn cell infection → lower motor neuron paralysis

Lower motor neuron paralysis means weakness with decreased reflexes and flaccid tone.

---

### One-line intuition

Enteroviruses start in the gut, survive the gut, then spread through blood to whatever tissue their subtype likes.

---

### High-yield details

They are:

* Picornaviridae family
* non-enveloped
* positive-sense single-stranded RNA
* fecal-oral transmission
* acid-stable, so they can survive the stomach

Non-enveloped viruses are generally tougher in the environment because they do not depend on a fragile lipid envelope.

---

## HY pearl

Enteroviruses are acid-stable picornaviruses, which helps explain fecal-oral spread: they can survive gastric acid and replicate in the GI tract.
