### Core idea

Drug-induced urinary retention means a medication makes it hard or impossible to empty the bladder.

So urine is made normally by the kidneys, reaches the bladder normally, but then the bladder cannot squeeze well enough or the outlet stays too tight.

Think:

* weak bladder contraction
* tight bladder neck/sphincter
* poor sensation that the bladder is full

Result:

* hesitancy = trouble starting urination
* weak stream
* feeling like the bladder is still full
* lower abdominal discomfort
* severe cases: cannot urinate at all

---

### Normal urination

To pee, you need two things to happen together:

* detrusor muscle contracts
* urethral sphincters relax

Detrusor muscle = smooth muscle in the bladder wall that squeezes urine out.

Urethral sphincters = ring-like muscles around the urethra that control urine flow.

So normal voiding is:

> bladder squeezes + outlet opens

Drug-induced retention happens when drugs mess up either side of that.

---

### Why anticholinergics cause retention

Anticholinergic drugs block acetylcholine.

Acetylcholine = the parasympathetic neurotransmitter that tells the detrusor muscle to contract.

So if you block acetylcholine:

* detrusor contracts less
* bladder cannot squeeze urine out
* urine stays behind

Classic drugs:

* diphenhydramine
* oxybutynin
* benztropine
* tricyclic antidepressants, or TCAs (older antidepressants like amitriptyline)
* antipsychotics with anticholinergic effects

Intuition:

> Anticholinergics make things dry and quiet: dry mouth, constipation, and a quiet bladder that will not squeeze.

---

### Why alpha agonists cause retention

Alpha-1 receptors tighten smooth muscle at the bladder neck and prostate.

So alpha agonists make the outlet clamp down.

Examples:

* pseudoephedrine
* phenylephrine

This is especially risky in someone with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia, meaning enlarged prostate).

Intuition:

> Alpha-1 stimulation tightens the urinary exit door.

---

### Other drug classes that can do it

Opioids can cause urinary retention because they:

* decrease bladder sensation
* reduce detrusor contraction
* increase sphincter tone

Benzodiazepines can contribute by relaxing/sedating the nervous system and weakening coordinated voiding.

NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, like ibuprofen) can sometimes contribute because prostaglandins help support bladder contraction.

Calcium channel blockers can reduce smooth muscle contraction, including bladder contraction.

---

### High-risk patients

Drug-induced urinary retention is more likely in:

* older men
* people with BPH (benign prostatic hyperplasia)
* people taking multiple anticholinergic drugs
* people after surgery/anesthesia
* people with neurologic disease affecting bladder control

Why BPH matters:

* prostate already narrows the urethra
* then a drug weakens bladder squeeze or tightens the outlet
* now urine cannot get out

---

## One-line intuition

Drug-induced urinary retention is when a medication turns down the bladder squeeze or tightens the urinary outlet, so urine gets trapped in the bladder.

---

## Pearl

For Step 1, the most HY mechanism is:

> anticholinergics block muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, so the detrusor muscle cannot contract well.

Classic clue: older patient starts diphenhydramine or a TCA (tricyclic antidepressant) and develops urinary hesitancy/retention plus dry mouth and constipation.
