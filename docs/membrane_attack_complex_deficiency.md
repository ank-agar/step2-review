# Membrane attack complex deficiency

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/60/Membrane_Attack_Complex_%28Terminal_Complement_Complex_C5b-9%29.png)

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/media/images/Nmeningitidis.jpg)

### Core idea

Membrane attack complex deficiency means the terminal complement pathway cannot form the MAC (membrane attack complex).

MAC = C5b, C6, C7, C8, and C9 forming a pore in bacterial membranes.

So if C5-C9 are deficient:

* complement can still tag/inflame pretty well
* but it cannot punch holes in certain bacteria

---

### Why Neisseria infections happen

Neisseria species are especially vulnerable to MAC killing because they are gram-negative diplococci (paired round bacteria with an outer membrane).

The MAC normally inserts into the outer membrane and makes a pore:

* ions/water rush in
* membrane integrity is lost
* bacteria lyse

If MAC is missing, Neisseria survives better.

Classic infections:

* recurrent Neisseria meningitidis meningitis
* recurrent Neisseria gonorrhoeae infection

Meningitis = inflammation/infection of the meninges (brain and spinal cord coverings).

---

### How to think about the complement pathway

The 3 upstream pathways are:

* classical pathway
* lectin pathway
* alternative pathway

They all converge at C3, then C5, then MAC.

So terminal pathway deficiency is basically:

> The alarm system still works, but the final bacterial hole-puncher is broken.

---

### Testing clue

CH50 (total hemolytic complement assay) is low in terminal complement deficiency.

Why?

CH50 tests whether the classical pathway can go all the way to red blood cell lysis.

If C5-C9 are missing, the cascade cannot finish the lysis step, so CH50 drops.

---

## One-line intuition

MAC deficiency = no C5b-9 pore = recurrent Neisseria infections.

---

## HY pearl

Recurrent Neisseria meningitidis infection should make you think terminal complement deficiency, especially C5-C9 deficiency.
