## Angiostrongylus cantonensis

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/angiostrongyliasis/modules/Angiostrong_LifeCycle.gif?_=01481)

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/angiostrongyliasis/images/4/Angio2_dic_400x.jpg?_=01491)

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/dpdx/angiostrongyliasis/images/5/HI_slug4_200x_annot.jpg?_=01510)

Angiostrongylus cantonensis is the rat lungworm.

It is a nematode (roundworm) that classically causes eosinophilic meningitis.

Eosinophilic meningitis means:

* eosinophils = parasite/allergy-associated white blood cells
* meningitis = inflammation of the meninges (brain and spinal cord coverings)

So the key idea is:

> a worm infection that irritates the meninges and causes lots of eosinophils in the CSF (cerebrospinal fluid).

---

### Life cycle intuition

Normal life cycle:

* rats are the definitive host (host where the adult parasite reproduces)
* snails/slugs are intermediate hosts (host where larvae develop)
* humans are accidental hosts

Humans usually get infected by eating:

* raw/undercooked snails or slugs
* contaminated produce with tiny infected snail/slug material
* sometimes infected paratenic hosts (transport hosts), like freshwater shrimp or crabs

---

### Why it causes meningitis

The infective L3 larvae (third-stage larvae) enter the human GI tract (gastrointestinal tract), then migrate.

But humans are the wrong host.

So instead of completing the rat life cycle, larvae migrate to the CNS (central nervous system), especially the brain/meninges, and die there.

That causes inflammation:

* headache
* neck stiffness
* nausea/vomiting
* paresthesias (tingling/abnormal sensation)
* eosinophils in CSF

---

### One-line intuition

Rat lungworm is a snail/slug-transmitted nematode that accidentally enters humans and wanders into the brain, causing eosinophilic meningitis.

---

## Pearl 🔥

For Step 1, if you see meningitis + eosinophils in CSF after eating raw snails/slugs or contaminated produce, think Angiostrongylus cantonensis.
