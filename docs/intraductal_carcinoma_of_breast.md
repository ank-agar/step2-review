### Core idea

Intraductal carcinoma of the breast usually means **ductal carcinoma in situ**, or **DCIS** (ductal carcinoma in situ).

Break it down:

* **intra-** = inside
* **ductal** = milk ducts
* **carcinoma** = malignant epithelial tumor
* **in situ** = still in its original place

So DCIS means:

> malignant cells are growing inside the breast ducts but have not invaded through the basement membrane.

---

### Why it is “in situ”

The key barrier is the **basement membrane** (thin supportive layer under epithelial cells).

In DCIS:

* cancer cells fill the duct
* basement membrane stays intact
* myoepithelial cells may still outline the duct
* no true stromal invasion

That is why DCIS is considered **noninvasive breast cancer**, often called **stage 0**.

---

### Why calcifications happen

DCIS often has central necrosis, especially **comedo necrosis** (dead tumor cells in the center of a duct).

Dead cells can calcify, so mammography may show:

* clustered microcalcifications

This is why DCIS is often found on screening mammogram before a palpable mass forms.

---

### Why it matters

DCIS itself has not invaded yet.

But if tumor cells break through the basement membrane into surrounding stroma, it becomes:

* **invasive ductal carcinoma** (ductal breast cancer that has invaded surrounding tissue)

So the whole concept is:

> trapped inside duct = DCIS; escaped through basement membrane = invasive carcinoma.

---

## One-line intuition

DCIS is breast cancer cells stuck inside the milk duct, like a duct packed with malignant cells that have not broken through the wall yet.

---

## Pearl 🔥

DCIS commonly presents as **microcalcifications on mammogram** due to necrosis/calcification inside ducts; **lobular carcinoma in situ**, or **LCIS** (lobular carcinoma in situ), is usually an incidental biopsy finding and is not classically linked to calcifications.
