### Core idea

Peripheral arterial disease, or PAD (peripheral arterial disease), is atherosclerosis (cholesterol plaque buildup in arteries) in the peripheral arteries, especially the leg arteries.

So blood cannot flow well to the legs.

Most common symptom:

* leg pain with walking
* relieved by rest

This is called intermittent claudication (episodic muscle pain from inadequate blood flow during activity).

---

### Why walking causes pain

At rest, the leg muscles do not need much oxygen.

But when walking, muscle oxygen demand increases.

If the artery is narrowed by plaque:

* not enough blood reaches the muscle
* muscle becomes relatively ischemic (low oxygen from low blood flow)
* pain/cramping starts

When the patient rests:

* oxygen demand falls
* available blood flow becomes enough again
* pain improves

---

### Classic findings

PAD can cause:

* decreased pulses
* cool extremity
* shiny skin
* hair loss on legs
* nonhealing ulcers
* pain with walking
* severe cases: pain at rest

Rest pain is bad because it means blood flow is so poor that even baseline tissue oxygen demand is not being met.

---

### Risk factors

Think systemic atherosclerosis risk factors:

* smoking
* diabetes mellitus
* hypertension (high blood pressure)
* hyperlipidemia (high blood lipids/cholesterol)
* older age

PAD is basically a marker that atherosclerosis is happening elsewhere too, including coronary arteries (heart arteries) and carotid arteries (neck arteries supplying brain).

---

### Diagnosis

Key test:

* ABI (ankle-brachial index)

ABI (ankle-brachial index) compares:

* ankle systolic blood pressure
* brachial artery systolic blood pressure in the arm

If ankle pressure is low compared with arm pressure, that suggests poor arterial flow to the leg.

Low ABI = PAD.

---

## One-line intuition

PAD is “coronary artery disease of the legs”: atherosclerotic plaque narrows leg arteries, so walking makes the muscles outstrip their blood supply.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Claudication from PAD improves with rest, while neurogenic claudication from spinal stenosis often improves with bending forward because flexion opens the spinal canal.
