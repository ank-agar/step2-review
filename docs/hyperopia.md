## Hyperopia

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Hypermetropia.svg/500px-Hypermetropia.svg.png)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a1/Hyperopia-GM.svg/500px-Hyperopia-GM.svg.png)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/Hyperopia_comparison.jpg/960px-Hyperopia_comparison.jpg)

Hyperopia means **farsightedness**.

The eye can see far objects better than near objects because light focuses **behind the retina** instead of directly on it.

Retina = the light-sensing layer in the back of the eye.

---

### Core idea

The eye is usually:

* too short from front to back
* or has too little refractive power

Refractive power = how strongly the cornea/lens bends light.

So the light does not bend enough, and the focal point lands behind the retina.

---

### Why near vision is blurry

Near objects require the lens to bend light more strongly.

In hyperopia, the eye already under-bends light, so near objects are especially hard to focus.

That causes:

* blurry near vision
* eye strain
* headaches with reading

---

### Correction

Hyperopia is corrected with a **convex/converging lens**.

Convex lens = lens that bends light rays inward before they enter the eye.

This moves the focal point forward onto the retina.

---

## One-line intuition

Hyperopia = the eye is too short or too weak at bending light, so images focus **behind** the retina.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Hyperopia uses a **convex lens**, while myopia (nearsightedness) uses a **concave lens**.

Easy memory:

* hyperopia = focus is too far back → bring it forward with convex lens
* myopia = focus is too far forward → push it back with concave lens
