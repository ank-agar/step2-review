### Core idea

An abdominal wall defect is a congenital defect (birth defect) where the anterior abdominal wall (front belly wall) does not close correctly, so abdominal organs can protrude outside the body.

The 2 big Step 1 versions are:

* **Omphalocele:** organs herniate through the umbilical ring and are covered by a membrane
* **Gastroschisis:** bowel herniates through a defect next to the umbilicus (belly button), usually right-sided, with no covering membrane

---

### Key difference

Think:

* **Omphalocele = on the umbilicus + covered**
* **Gastroschisis = gap beside the umbilicus + gut exposed**

Why that matters:

* Omphalocele is a midline developmental problem, so it is more associated with other congenital anomalies and chromosomal disorders
* Gastroschisis is usually an isolated abdominal wall problem, so the bowel is exposed directly to amniotic fluid (fluid around the fetus), causing inflammation and thickening

---

### Why the findings happen

* **Organs outside the abdomen:** the abdominal wall failed to close normally
* **High AFP (alpha-fetoprotein):** fetal proteins leak into amniotic fluid and maternal blood when the body wall is open
* **Gastroschisis bowel damage:** no protective sac, so bowel sits directly in amniotic fluid
* **Omphalocele associations:** midline embryologic defect often travels with other developmental abnormalities

---

### Classic associations

Omphalocele is associated with:

* trisomy 13
* trisomy 18
* Beckwith-Wiedemann syndrome
* cardiac defects

Gastroschisis is associated with:

* young maternal age
* isolated bowel problems, like atresia (missing/closed segment of intestine)

---

## One-line intuition

Abdominal wall defect means the baby’s belly wall did not close, and Step 1 mostly wants you to separate **omphalocele = covered, midline, more syndromic** from **gastroschisis = uncovered, lateral, usually isolated**.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Both omphalocele and gastroschisis can increase maternal serum AFP (alpha-fetoprotein), but **omphalocele is the one classically linked to trisomies and other congenital anomalies**.
