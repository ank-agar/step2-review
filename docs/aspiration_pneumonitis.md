### Core idea

Aspiration pneumonitis = acute lung inflammation after aspirating acidic gastric contents.

Aspiration means material goes into the airway/lungs instead of the esophagus (food tube).

Pneumonitis means lung inflammation.

So:

> stomach acid goes down the wrong pipe and chemically burns the lung.

---

### Why it happens

Normally, the epiglottis (flap that covers the airway during swallowing) protects the trachea (windpipe).

If someone has impaired consciousness, the airway reflexes are weaker, so vomit or stomach contents can enter the lungs.

Common settings:

* alcohol intoxication
* anesthesia
* seizure
* stroke
* coma
* sedation

---

### Why the lung gets inflamed

Gastric acid is very irritating.

When acid reaches the alveoli (tiny air sacs where gas exchange happens), it damages the alveolar lining and triggers inflammation.

That causes:

* cough
* dyspnea (shortness of breath)
* hypoxemia (low blood oxygen)
* fever sometimes
* patchy infiltrates on CXR (chest x-ray)

The key is that this is chemical injury first, not necessarily infection.

---

### Aspiration pneumonitis vs aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration pneumonitis:

* chemical inflammation
* usually from sterile gastric acid
* abrupt onset after vomiting/aspiration
* antibiotics are not always needed initially

Aspiration pneumonia:

* bacterial infection
* often from aspirated oral bacteria
* more gradual or persistent symptoms
* antibiotics are needed

Simple way:

* pneumonitis = chemical burn
* pneumonia = infection

---

### One-line intuition

Aspiration pneumonitis is the lung reacting like it got splashed with acid.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Aspiration affects dependent lung segments:

* upright patient: lower lobes, especially right lower lobe
* supine patient: posterior upper lobes or superior lower lobes

Why right side? The right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical, so aspirated material more easily falls into it.
