## Peptic ulcer perforation

### Core idea

Peptic ulcer perforation = a peptic ulcer (acid-related sore in the stomach or duodenum) burns all the way through the wall.

So now stomach/duodenal contents leak into the peritoneal cavity (space around abdominal organs).

That causes:

* sudden severe abdominal pain
* rigid abdomen
* peritonitis (inflammation of the peritoneum)
* free air under the diaphragm on X-ray

---

### Why it happens

Normally, an ulcer is just a hole in the mucosa (inner lining).

If the ulcer keeps digging deeper:

* mucosa gets destroyed
* submucosa gets destroyed
* muscle wall gets destroyed
* then it fully perforates

Once there is a full-thickness hole, air and acidic GI (gastrointestinal) contents escape.

That is why the pain is sudden and intense: acid is now irritating the peritoneum, which is very pain-sensitive.

---

### Classic causes

Most commonly from:

* H. pylori (Helicobacter pylori) infection
* NSAIDs (nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), like ibuprofen/naproxen

NSAIDs reduce prostaglandins (protective molecules that help maintain gastric mucus and blood flow), so the stomach lining becomes easier for acid to injure.

---

### Imaging clue

Perforation can cause pneumoperitoneum (free air in the peritoneal cavity).

On upright chest/abdominal X-ray, this can show up as:

* free air under the diaphragm

Intuition:

Air is supposed to stay inside the GI tract. If you see it outside the GI tract, think perforation.

---

## One-line intuition

A peptic ulcer digs through the full stomach/duodenal wall, leaking acid and air into the abdomen, causing acute peritonitis.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Anterior duodenal ulcers are more likely to perforate, while posterior duodenal ulcers are more likely to bleed by eroding the gastroduodenal artery.
