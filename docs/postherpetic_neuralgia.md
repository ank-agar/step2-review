### Core idea

Postherpetic neuralgia is chronic nerve pain after shingles.

Break it down:

* post- = after
* herpetic = related to herpes zoster
* neuralgia = nerve pain

So:

> pain that persists after a shingles outbreak.

---

### What causes it

Shingles is reactivation of VZV (varicella-zoster virus), the virus that causes chickenpox.

After chickenpox, VZV hides dormant in dorsal root ganglia (sensory nerve cell body clusters near the spinal cord).

Later, it can reactivate and travel down a sensory nerve to the skin, causing:

* painful vesicular rash
* usually in one dermatome (skin region supplied by one spinal nerve)
* usually on one side of the body

If the virus damages the sensory nerve, pain can continue even after the rash heals.

That persistent pain is PHN (postherpetic neuralgia).

---

### Why the pain happens

The nerve got injured, so it keeps sending pain signals even when the skin no longer has active rash.

That is why patients can have:

* burning pain
* stabbing pain
* tingling
* allodynia (pain from something normally nonpainful, like clothing touching the skin)

Think:

> rash is gone, but the nerve wiring is still irritated/damaged.

---

### Classic association

Most common after shingles in older adults.

Risk increases with:

* age
* severe shingles pain
* severe rash
* delayed antiviral treatment
* immunosuppression (weakened immune system)

---

### Treatment idea

Since this is neuropathic pain (pain from nerve injury), regular pain meds may not work well.

Common treatments include:

* gabapentin or pregabalin
* tricyclic antidepressants like amitriptyline
* topical lidocaine patches
* capsaicin cream/patches

---

## One-line intuition

Postherpetic neuralgia is when shingles damages a sensory nerve, so pain continues in that dermatome after the skin rash resolves.

---

## HY pearl

Shingles and postherpetic neuralgia are prevented by the zoster vaccine, and acute shingles is treated with antivirals like acyclovir, valacyclovir, or famciclovir, especially if started within 72 hours of rash onset.
