Epistaxis = nosebleed.

It literally means bleeding from the nasal mucosa (the moist lining inside the nose).

Most cases are anterior epistaxis (bleeding from the front of the nose), especially from:

* Kiesselbach plexus
* also called Little area

Plexus = network of vessels.

So Kiesselbach plexus is basically a fragile blood vessel crossroads on the anterior nasal septum (front part of the wall between the nostrils).

---

### Core idea

The front nasal septum has a lot of tiny vessels close to the surface.

That area gets:

* dry air
* irritation
* nose picking/trauma
* inflammation from upper respiratory infections

So the vessels crack open easily.

That is why most nosebleeds are from the front of the nose and are usually not dangerous.

---

### Why anterior nosebleeds are common

Kiesselbach plexus gets blood from branches of both:

* internal carotid artery system
* external carotid artery system

Carotid arteries = major arteries that supply the head and neck.

So it is a high-blood-flow area sitting right at the dry, trauma-prone front of the nose.

Intuition:

> lots of fragile vessels + dry air + easy trauma = nosebleed.

---

### Anterior vs posterior epistaxis

Anterior epistaxis:

* most common
* usually from Kiesselbach plexus
* blood comes out the front of the nostril
* usually easier to stop with pressure

Posterior epistaxis:

* less common
* deeper/back of nose
* more common in older adults
* can bleed into the throat
* more serious and harder to control

Posterior = farther back.

---

### Management intuition

First step is direct pressure:

* sit upright
* lean forward
* pinch the soft lower part of the nose
* hold pressure continuously

Lean forward so blood does not run backward into the throat and get swallowed.

If pressure fails, clinicians may use:

* topical vasoconstrictor (medicine that narrows blood vessels)
* cautery (burning/sealing the bleeding vessel)
* nasal packing

---

## One-line intuition

Epistaxis is usually a front-of-the-nose bleed from Kiesselbach plexus because that vessel-rich area is superficial, dry, and easy to traumatize.

---

## Pearl 🔥

For Step 1, recurrent epistaxis + mucocutaneous telangiectasias (small dilated blood vessels on skin/mucosa) points to hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia, also called Osler-Weber-Rendu syndrome.
