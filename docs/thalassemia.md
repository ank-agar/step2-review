### Core idea

Thalassemia is an inherited disorder where you make too little globin chain.

Globin chain = the protein part of hemoglobin (oxygen-carrying protein in RBCs, or red blood cells).

Normal adult hemoglobin A (HbA, adult hemoglobin) is:

* 2 alpha chains
* 2 beta chains

So thalassemia is basically:

> normal hemoglobin ingredients, but one ingredient is underproduced.

---

### Alpha vs beta

**Alpha thalassemia** = decreased alpha-globin production.

**Beta thalassemia** = decreased beta-globin production.

The problem is not a weird-shaped hemoglobin like sickle cell disease.

The problem is:

* not enough chain production
* chain imbalance
* fragile RBCs
* anemia

---

### Why the anemia happens

If one globin chain is missing, the other chain is left unpaired.

Those unpaired chains damage RBC precursors in the bone marrow.

That causes:

* ineffective erythropoiesis (bone marrow tries to make RBCs, but many die before maturing)
* hemolysis (RBC destruction)
* microcytic anemia (small RBC anemia)

Microcytic = low MCV (mean corpuscular volume), meaning the RBCs are small.

Intuition:

> If hemoglobin assembly is broken, RBCs come out small, pale, and fragile.

---

### Classic smear finding

Thalassemia can show:

* target cells
* microcytosis (small RBCs)
* hypochromia (pale RBCs from low hemoglobin)

Target cells happen because the RBC has too much membrane surface area relative to the amount of hemoglobin inside.

So it looks like a bullseye:

* outer rim of hemoglobin
* pale ring
* central dot of hemoglobin

---

### Beta thalassemia major

Beta thalassemia major is severe because beta-chain production is very low or absent.

After birth, babies switch from fetal hemoglobin (HbF, fetal hemoglobin) to adult hemoglobin (HbA, adult hemoglobin).

HbF uses gamma chains instead of beta chains.

So symptoms often appear after around 6 months, when the baby needs beta chains for HbA.

Findings:

* severe anemia
* jaundice from hemolysis
* hepatosplenomegaly (big liver and spleen)
* bone marrow expansion
* crew-cut skull on x-ray
* iron overload from transfusions and increased iron absorption

---

### Why bone changes happen

The body senses anemia and increases EPO (erythropoietin, the hormone that tells bone marrow to make RBCs).

Bone marrow works harder and expands.

That expansion can cause skeletal changes, especially in severe beta thalassemia.

So:

> low hemoglobin -> high EPO -> marrow expansion -> bone deformities.

---

### Electrophoresis pattern

In beta thalassemia:

* HbA decreases
* HbA2 increases
* HbF increases

HbA2 (hemoglobin A2) is made of 2 alpha chains and 2 delta chains.

Why HbA2 goes up:

If beta chains are low, the body uses relatively more delta and gamma chains.

---

## One-line intuition

Thalassemia = not enough alpha or beta globin chains, so hemoglobin assembly fails and RBCs become small, pale, fragile target cells.

---

## Pearl

Beta thalassemia trait is a classic cause of microcytic anemia with a normal or increased RBC count, while iron deficiency anemia usually has a decreased RBC count.
