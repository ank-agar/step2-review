Amaurosis fugax means temporary, painless vision loss, classically in one eye.

Break it down:

* amaurosis = darkness/vision loss
* fugax = fleeting

So literally:

> fleeting blindness

---

### Core idea

Think of it as a retinal TIA (transient ischemic attack = temporary loss of blood flow without permanent infarction).

Most classic cause:

* atherosclerotic plaque in the ipsilateral carotid artery
* small embolus (traveling clot/cholesterol fragment) goes to the ophthalmic artery or retinal artery
* retina briefly loses blood flow
* vision goes dark, then returns when flow comes back

Ipsilateral means same side.

So left carotid disease can cause temporary vision loss in the left eye.

---

### What the patient says

Classically:

* "a curtain came down over one eye"
* painless
* lasts seconds to minutes
* vision returns fully

Why one eye?

Because the problem is usually before the optic chiasm (where optic nerve fibers cross), at the retina/optic nerve blood supply.

---

### Why it matters

Even though the vision comes back, this is a warning sign.

It can mean the same embolic process that briefly blocked the retinal circulation could next block a cerebral artery and cause a stroke.

So amaurosis fugax is not just an eye symptom; it is a vascular red flag.

---

### Common causes to remember

* carotid artery atherosclerosis
* cardiac emboli, like atrial fibrillation (irregular atrial rhythm causing clot formation)
* giant cell arteritis in older patients
* retinal vasospasm/migraine-like causes

Giant cell arteritis clues:

* older patient
* headache
* jaw claudication (jaw pain with chewing)
* scalp tenderness
* high ESR (erythrocyte sedimentation rate = inflammation marker)

---

## One-line intuition

Amaurosis fugax is a mini-stroke of the retina: temporary retinal ischemia causes temporary monocular darkness.

---

## HY pearl

Amaurosis fugax localizes to the ipsilateral carotid/ophthalmic-retinal circulation and should make you think:

* carotid stenosis
* embolus
* stroke risk
