### Core idea

Renal insufficiency means the kidneys are not filtering blood well enough.

Renal = kidney  
Insufficiency = not enough function

So basically:

> kidney function is reduced, but it does not always mean complete kidney failure yet.

---

### What the kidney normally does

The kidneys normally:

* filter blood
* remove waste products
* balance water/electrolytes
* regulate blood pressure
* help make erythropoietin, or EPO (a hormone that stimulates red blood cell production)
* activate vitamin D

When kidney function drops, all of those jobs can get impaired.

---

### Why the findings happen

If filtration decreases:

* creatinine rises because creatinine is normally cleared by the kidney
* blood urea nitrogen, or BUN (nitrogen waste from protein breakdown), rises
* potassium can rise because kidneys normally excrete potassium
* fluid can build up because kidneys cannot remove enough water/sodium
* metabolic acidosis can happen because kidneys cannot excrete acid well
* anemia can happen because damaged kidneys make less EPO

The best way to think about it:

> fewer working nephrons = less filtering capacity.

Nephrons are the tiny filtering units of the kidney.

---

### Acute vs chronic

Renal insufficiency can be:

* acute: sudden drop in kidney function
* chronic: gradual long-term loss of kidney function

Chronic kidney disease, or CKD (chronic kidney disease), is usually tracked with eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate).

Lower eGFR = worse kidney filtration.

---

## One-line intuition

Renal insufficiency is when the kidney filter is underperforming, so waste, acid, potassium, and fluid start to accumulate.

---

## Pearl

HY Step 1 pearl: chronic renal insufficiency causes secondary hyperparathyroidism because damaged kidneys retain phosphate and make less active vitamin D, causing low calcium and increased parathyroid hormone, or PTH (parathyroid hormone).
