### Core idea

Delirium is an **acute, fluctuating change in attention and awareness**.

In plain English:

* the patient suddenly becomes confused
* they cannot focus well
* their mental status changes over hours to days
* symptoms often wax and wane, meaning they get better and worse throughout the day

This is different from dementia, which is usually **chronic and progressive** over months to years.

---

### The key word is attention

Delirium is not just “memory loss.”

The big Step 1 clue is:

* patient cannot maintain attention
* gets distracted easily
* cannot follow conversation
* has fluctuating alertness

So if someone is acutely confused after surgery, infection, medication change, or hospitalization, think delirium.

---

### Why delirium happens

The brain is temporarily not functioning normally because of an underlying stressor.

Common triggers:

* infection, especially UTI (urinary tract infection) or pneumonia
* medications, especially anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids
* alcohol withdrawal
* electrolyte abnormalities
* hypoxia (low oxygen)
* pain
* sleep deprivation
* urinary retention or constipation
* ICU (intensive care unit) hospitalization

Reasoning:

The brain needs stable oxygen, glucose, sleep, neurotransmitters, and inflammation control. If the body gets sick or drugs disrupt neurotransmitters, cortical brain function becomes disorganized, so attention and awareness fluctuate.

---

### Neurotransmitter intuition

Classic association:

* ↓ acetylcholine
* ↑ dopamine

Acetylcholine helps with attention and cognition. So anticholinergic drugs can push an older patient into delirium.

Dopamine excess can contribute to agitation, hallucinations, and psychosis-like symptoms.

---

### Types

Delirium can be:

* **hyperactive:** agitated, restless, hallucinating
* **hypoactive:** sleepy, quiet, withdrawn
* **mixed:** switches between both

Important: hypoactive delirium is easy to miss because the patient may just look “tired” or depressed.

---

### Delirium vs dementia

High-yield difference:

* delirium = acute, fluctuating, poor attention
* dementia = chronic, progressive, memory-predominant

Another way:

Delirium is like the brain’s Wi-Fi signal cutting in and out. Dementia is like the hardware slowly wearing down.

---

## One-line intuition

Delirium is an acute reversible brain dysfunction where the patient’s attention and awareness fluctuate because the brain is being stressed by illness, drugs, withdrawal, or metabolic problems.

---

## Pearl 🔥

For Step 1, sudden confusion in an older hospitalized patient is **delirium until proven otherwise**, and the classic medication risk is **anticholinergic drugs** because they decrease acetylcholine, which is needed for attention.
