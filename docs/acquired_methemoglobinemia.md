# Acquired methemoglobinemia

Acquired methemoglobinemia is when a drug/chemical oxidizes hemoglobin iron from:

* Fe2+ = ferrous iron, normal oxygen-binding form
* Fe3+ = ferric iron, methemoglobin form

Methemoglobin cannot carry oxygen well.

So even if the lungs are bringing in oxygen, the blood cannot deliver that oxygen properly to tissues.

---

### Common causes

Think oxidizing drugs/chemicals:

* benzocaine or lidocaine, which are local anesthetics
* nitrates/nitrites
* dapsone
* sulfonamides
* primaquine

Acquired means it was caused by an exposure, not an inherited enzyme problem.

---

### Why cyanosis happens

Cyanosis means bluish discoloration from poor oxygen delivery.

In methemoglobinemia:

* Fe3+ hemoglobin cannot bind oxygen
* remaining Fe2+ hemoglobin holds onto oxygen too tightly
* oxygen unloading to tissues decreases

So this is a functional anemia.

Functional anemia means hemoglobin may be present, but it is not functioning correctly.

---

### Classic clue

The patient has cyanosis and low SpO2 (oxygen saturation by pulse oximeter), but oxygen does not fix it well.

Why?

Because the problem is not lack of oxygen entering the lungs.

The problem is abnormal hemoglobin that cannot carry/release oxygen normally.

Also classic:

* chocolate-brown blood
* saturation gap

Saturation gap means PaO2 (arterial oxygen dissolved in plasma) may be normal/high, but SpO2 (pulse oximeter oxygen saturation) stays low.

---

### Treatment

Treat with methylene blue.

Methylene blue helps reduce:

* Fe3+ back to Fe2+
* methemoglobin back to normal hemoglobin

But be careful in G6PD deficiency (glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency), because methylene blue needs NADPH (reduced nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate) to work and can worsen hemolysis.

---

## One-line intuition

Acquired methemoglobinemia is basically oxidized hemoglobin: Fe2+ becomes Fe3+, so blood looks brown and cannot deliver oxygen properly.

---
