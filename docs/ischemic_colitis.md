## Ischemic colitis

![Image](https://www.gastrointestinalatlas.com/imagenes/headers/Ischemic_Colitis.jpg)

### Core idea

Ischemic colitis = inflammation/injury of the colon due to decreased blood flow.

Break it down:

* ischemic = low blood/oxygen supply
* colitis = inflammation of the colon

So the colon is basically getting “under-perfused” and becomes irritated/injured.

---

### Why it happens

The colon has vulnerable “watershed” areas.

Watershed area = a border zone between 2 arterial blood supplies, so it has weaker backup blood flow.

Classic sites:

* splenic flexure
* rectosigmoid junction

Why these areas?

* splenic flexure is between SMA (superior mesenteric artery) and IMA (inferior mesenteric artery) supply
* rectosigmoid junction is between IMA and internal iliac artery supply

So when blood pressure drops or blood flow is reduced, these border zones get hit first.

---

### Typical presentation

Usually older patient with vascular disease who develops:

* crampy abdominal pain
* bloody diarrhea or hematochezia (bright red blood per rectum)
* tenderness, often left-sided

Common triggers/risk factors:

* hypotension (low blood pressure)
* atherosclerosis (plaque buildup in arteries)
* shock
* heart failure
* recent aortic surgery

---

### Intuition

Think:

> low-flow state + weak colon border-zone blood supply = mucosal injury + bleeding.

The mucosa (inner lining) is very metabolically active, so it gets injured early when oxygen drops.

That is why patients can bleed: the colon lining becomes damaged and sloughs off.

---

### Step 1 contrast

Ischemic colitis usually affects watershed areas of the colon.

Acute mesenteric ischemia often involves the small bowel and is more classically due to an embolus to the SMA (superior mesenteric artery), causing severe pain “out of proportion” to exam.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Rectal involvement is uncommon in ischemic colitis because the rectum has rich collateral blood supply from multiple arteries.
