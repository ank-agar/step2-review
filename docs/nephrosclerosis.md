### Core idea

Nephrosclerosis = “hardening/scarring of the kidney.”

Usually this means chronic hypertension (high blood pressure) damages small renal arteries/arterioles (small arteries), causing:

* vessel wall thickening
* narrowed lumen (inside opening of the vessel)
* decreased renal blood flow
* ischemic atrophy (shrinkage from low blood supply)
* glomerulosclerosis (scarring of glomeruli, the kidney filtering tufts)

---

### Why hypertension causes it

High pressure keeps injuring small renal vessels.

The vessel responds by laying down hyaline material (pink protein-like material on histology) in the wall.

That makes the wall thicker and the lumen smaller.

Smaller lumen = less blood reaches the nephron (kidney filtering unit).

Less blood = chronic ischemia (low oxygen) = kidney scarring.

---

### Benign vs malignant

**Benign nephrosclerosis** = long-standing, gradual hypertension.

Classic finding:

* hyaline arteriolosclerosis (pink hyaline thickening of small arteriole walls)
* small, finely granular kidneys

**Malignant nephrosclerosis** = severe acute hypertension.

Classic findings:

* hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis (concentric “onion-skin” vessel wall thickening)
* fibrinoid necrosis (necrotic vessel wall with fibrin-like protein leakage)
* “flea-bitten” kidney from tiny hemorrhages

---

### What you see clinically

Can cause chronic kidney disease (CKD, chronic kidney disease), with:

* slowly rising creatinine
* mild proteinuria (protein in urine)
* long history of hypertension
* small kidneys on imaging

---

## One-line intuition

High blood pressure slowly squeezes and scars the kidney’s tiny vessels, so the nephrons lose blood supply and the kidney becomes small and scarred.

---

## Pearl 🔥

For Step 1:  

**benign hypertension = hyaline arteriolosclerosis**  
**malignant hypertension = hyperplastic arteriolosclerosis + fibrinoid necrosis**
