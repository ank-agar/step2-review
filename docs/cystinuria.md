### Core idea

Cystinuria is an inherited kidney transporter defect where the proximal tubule (early kidney tubule segment that reabsorbs filtered stuff) cannot reabsorb certain amino acids (protein building blocks).

The amino acids lost in urine are:

* cystine
* ornithine
* lysine
* arginine

Mnemonic (memory trick):

> COLA

This happens because of defective amino acid transporters (membrane proteins that move amino acids back into blood) in the proximal tubule.

---

### Why stones form

Cystine is poorly soluble in urine, especially acidic urine.

So if too much cystine stays in the urine:

* cystine precipitates (comes out of solution as a solid)
* forms hexagonal crystals
* crystals grow into cystine kidney stones

The other amino acids in COLA are also lost, but they stay soluble, so they do not usually cause the problem.

---

### Classic findings

Think:

* recurrent kidney stones, often in childhood or young patients
* flank pain
* hematuria (blood in urine)
* hexagonal cystine crystals on urinalysis (urine test)
* positive cyanide-nitroprusside test (screening test for excess cystine)

Hexagonal crystals are the big clue.

---

### Treatment intuition

The goal is to keep cystine dissolved.

So treatment includes:

* lots of fluids to dilute urine
* alkalinize urine, usually with potassium citrate
* reduce sodium intake
* thiol drugs (sulfur-containing drugs that bind cystine) like tiopronin or penicillamine if severe

Alkalinizing works because cystine dissolves better at higher urine pH (measure of acidity/basicity).

---

## One-line intuition

Cystinuria = proximal tubule cannot reabsorb COLA amino acids, and cystine is the one that crashes out into hexagonal kidney stones.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Cystinuria is autosomal recessive (needs two mutated gene copies) and is associated with mutations in SLC3A1 or SLC7A9, which encode parts of the cystine/basic amino acid transporter.
