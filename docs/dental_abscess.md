## Dental abscess

![Image](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/images/ency/fullsize/9979.jpg)

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK589664/bin/Dental__abscess.jpg)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/48/Abces_parulique.jpg)

A dental abscess is a localized collection of pus from a bacterial tooth infection.

Pus = dead neutrophils (white blood cells), bacteria, and tissue debris.

Most commonly, bacteria enter through:

* dental caries (cavities)
* cracked tooth
* periodontal disease (gum disease)

Then the infection can spread into the pulp (soft neurovascular center of the tooth) and down to the root tip.

---

### Core idea

The tooth gets infected, pressure builds up, and pus collects near the root or gum.

That is why it causes:

* severe throbbing tooth pain
* swelling of the gum or face
* tenderness to chewing
* fever sometimes
* bad taste if it drains into the mouth

---

### Why it hurts so much

The pulp is trapped inside hard enamel/dentin (outer tooth layers), so swelling has nowhere to expand.

So pressure rises inside the tooth/root area, irritating nerves.

That pressure + inflammation = intense pain.

---

### Types

* Periapical abscess: infection at the apex (tip) of the tooth root, usually from tooth decay
* Periodontal abscess: infection in the gum/periodontal tissue, usually from gum disease

Periapical = around the apex (root tip).

Periodontal = around the tooth-supporting tissues.

---

### Treatment idea

Antibiotics alone usually are not enough because the pus pocket needs source control.

Source control = physically removing/draining the infected focus.

Treatment is usually:

* drainage
* root canal or tooth extraction
* antibiotics if spreading infection, fever, cellulitis (skin/soft tissue infection), or immunocompromise

---

## One-line intuition

A dental abscess is a trapped pus pocket from a tooth infection, and it hurts because pressure builds inside a tight, hard space.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Untreated dental abscess can spread into deep neck spaces and cause Ludwig angina, a life-threatening floor-of-mouth infection that can obstruct the airway.
