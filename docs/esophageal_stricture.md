### Core idea

An esophageal stricture is a narrowing of the esophagus.

The esophagus is the food tube that carries food from the mouth to the stomach.

So a stricture is basically:

> a scarred/tight segment of the food pipe that makes it harder for food to pass.

---

### Why it happens

Most commonly, chronic irritation causes inflammation, then healing with fibrosis.

Fibrosis means scar tissue formation.

Common causes:

* GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease): acid repeatedly injures the lower esophagus
* eosinophilic esophagitis: allergic-type inflammation with eosinophils, a type of white blood cell
* caustic ingestion: swallowing strong acids or bases
* radiation or prior procedures
* malignancy: cancer can physically narrow the lumen

Lumen means the hollow inside space of a tube.

---

### Why the symptoms happen

The main symptom is dysphagia, meaning difficulty swallowing.

The classic pattern:

* solids first
* then liquids later if it becomes severe

Why?

Solid food needs a wider opening to pass.

Liquids can squeeze through a smaller opening, so liquid dysphagia usually means the narrowing is more advanced or there is a motility problem.

Motility means coordinated muscle movement.

---

### Big test clue

If the question says:

* long history of GERD
* progressive solid-food dysphagia
* distal esophageal narrowing

Think:

> peptic esophageal stricture from chronic acid reflux.

Peptic here means acid-related.

---

## One-line intuition

Repeated injury makes the esophagus heal with scar tissue, and scar tissue contracts, so the food tube gets tight.

---

## Pearl

Progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids is concerning for mechanical obstruction, including esophageal cancer, but intermittent dysphagia to both solids and liquids from the start suggests a motility disorder like achalasia.
