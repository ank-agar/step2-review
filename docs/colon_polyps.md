### Core idea

Colon polyps are abnormal growths from the inner lining of the colon (large intestine).

Think:

* colon lining = mucosa (inner surface layer)
* polyp = extra growth sticking into the lumen (hollow inside space of the gut)

Most are benign (not cancer), but some can become colorectal cancer over time.

---

### Main types

* **Hyperplastic polyps:** usually low cancer risk, especially if small and left-sided
* **Adenomatous polyps/adenomas:** premalignant (can turn into cancer)
* **Sessile serrated polyps:** premalignant, often right-sided

Break it down:

* sessile = flat/broad-based
* pedunculated = on a stalk
* adenoma = benign gland-forming tumor with cancer potential

---

### Why they matter

The big concept is the adenoma-carcinoma sequence:

normal colon mucosa → adenoma → carcinoma (cancer)

This usually takes years, which is why colonoscopy can prevent cancer:

* find polyp
* remove polyp
* prevent progression to cancer

---

### Why bigger polyps are riskier

Bigger polyp = more cells = more chances for mutations to accumulate.

So cancer risk increases with:

* larger size
* villous histology (finger-like gland pattern)
* high-grade dysplasia (very abnormal cells, but not yet invasive cancer)

Dysplasia means abnormal cell growth/appearance.

---

### Symptoms

Usually asymptomatic (no symptoms).

If symptoms happen, they can include:

* occult blood in stool (hidden blood)
* visible rectal bleeding
* iron deficiency anemia (low iron causing low hemoglobin)
* change in bowel habits

---

## One-line intuition

A colon polyp is a little overgrowth of colon lining; the dangerous ones are important because they are often the removable precursor to colon cancer.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Familial adenomatous polyposis, or FAP (familial adenomatous polyposis), is due to an APC mutation (adenomatous polyposis coli tumor suppressor gene) and causes hundreds to thousands of colon polyps with near-100% colorectal cancer risk if untreated.
