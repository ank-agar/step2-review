### Core idea

Peroneal tunnel syndrome is compression of the common peroneal nerve, also called the common fibular nerve, near the fibular head/neck.

The common peroneal nerve wraps around the outside of the knee, close to the fibula, so it is very exposed there.

---

### Why it causes symptoms

The common peroneal nerve splits into:

* deep peroneal nerve: dorsiflexion, meaning lifting the foot upward
* superficial peroneal nerve: eversion, meaning turning the sole outward, plus sensation over much of the dorsum of the foot

So if the nerve is compressed, you can get:

* lateral leg pain
* numbness/tingling over the dorsum of the foot
* weak dorsiflexion
* foot drop, meaning the foot slaps down because you cannot lift it well
* difficulty heel-walking

---

### Why this nerve gets trapped

The nerve passes through a tight area near the fibular neck and peroneus longus muscle.

Anything that narrows or compresses that space can irritate the nerve:

* fibular fracture
* tight cast
* prolonged leg crossing
* weight loss, because less fat cushions the nerve
* ganglion cyst or mass
* repetitive knee/ankle movement

---

### Intuition

Think of the common peroneal nerve like a cable wrapping around the outside of the knee.

Because it hugs the fibular neck, even mild compression there can block the signal to the muscles that lift the foot.

So:

> peroneal nerve compression near fibular head = weak dorsiflexion = foot drop

---

### Key Step 1 distinction

Common peroneal nerve injury causes:

* foot drop
* sensory loss on dorsum of foot/lateral leg
* weak eversion

Tibial nerve injury is different:

* weak plantarflexion, meaning pushing the foot downward
* sensory loss on sole of foot

---

## Pearl 🔥

Foot drop after fibular neck fracture is classic for common peroneal nerve injury because the nerve winds superficially around the fibular neck.
