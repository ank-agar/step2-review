## C3 deficiency

![Image](https://www.immunology.org/sites/default/files/inline-images/Complement-System-Figure-1.png)

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/meningococcal/media/images/Nmeningitidis.jpg)

![Image](https://www.cdc.gov/pneumococcal/media/images/Pneumococcus.png)

C3 deficiency means low or absent C3 (complement component 3), which is the central protein of the complement system.

Complement system = blood proteins that help antibodies and immune cells kill microbes.

---

### Core idea

All 3 complement pathways converge on C3:

* classical pathway
* lectin pathway
* alternative pathway

So if C3 is missing, the whole complement system loses its main “hub.”

---

### What C3 normally does

C3 gets split into:

* C3a = inflammation signal
* C3b = opsonin

Opsonin means a “tag” that marks bacteria for phagocytosis (eating by neutrophils/macrophages).

So C3b is basically:

> “Hey immune cells, eat this bacteria.”

---

### Why infections happen

Without C3:

* poor opsonization (bacteria are not tagged well)
* poor phagocytosis (immune cells cannot grab/eat bacteria efficiently)
* impaired MAC (membrane attack complex) formation downstream

This causes recurrent severe pyogenic infections.

Pyogenic means pus-forming, usually from extracellular bacteria.

Classic organisms:

* Streptococcus pneumoniae
* Neisseria meningitidis
* Haemophilus influenzae

---

### Why encapsulated bacteria are high risk

Encapsulated bacteria have a slippery capsule that makes them hard for phagocytes to grab.

They need opsonins like:

* IgG (immunoglobulin G)
* C3b

So if C3b is missing, encapsulated bugs can evade immune clearance.

---

## One-line intuition

C3 is the main complement “tagging” protein, so C3 deficiency means bacteria do not get tagged well for immune cells to eat.

---

## Pearl 🔥

C3 deficiency causes severe recurrent pyogenic infections, while terminal complement deficiencies C5-C9 classically cause recurrent Neisseria infections.
