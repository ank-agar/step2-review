### Core idea

Granulomatous enteritis basically means:

* granulomatous = forming granulomas (organized clusters of macrophages, which are immune cells that "wall off" something)
* enteritis = inflammation of the small intestine

So:

> chronic small bowel inflammation with granulomas.

Most Step 1 association:

* **Crohn disease** = chronic inflammatory bowel disease (IBD, immune-mediated gut inflammation) with **noncaseating granulomas**

Noncaseating means the granuloma does **not** have cheesy necrosis in the center.

---

### Why granulomas form

Granulomas happen when the immune system is dealing with something it cannot easily clear.

So instead of quick inflammation, macrophages gather into a tight cluster and try to contain the problem.

In Crohn disease, this immune reaction causes:

* chronic inflammation
* granulomas
* thickened bowel wall
* ulceration
* strictures (narrowed bowel segments)
* fistulas (abnormal connections between organs)

---

### Why Crohn causes deep bowel damage

Crohn inflammation is **transmural**.

Transmural means it goes through the full thickness of the bowel wall.

That is why Crohn can cause:

* fistulas
* strictures
* creeping fat
* obstruction

Compare that with ulcerative colitis (UC, inflammatory bowel disease limited to colon mucosa/submucosa), which is more superficial and continuous.

---

### Classic Crohn pattern

Crohn disease often has:

* skip lesions = diseased bowel with normal bowel in between
* terminal ileum involvement = inflammation near the end of the small intestine
* noncaseating granulomas
* abdominal pain
* diarrhea
* weight loss

The terminal ileum matters because it absorbs:

* vitamin B12
* bile acids

So Crohn can cause:

* B12 deficiency
* bile acid diarrhea
* gallstones from bile salt malabsorption

---

## One-line intuition

Granulomatous enteritis is basically Crohn-style small bowel inflammation: the immune system forms granulomas and damages the bowel wall deeply.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Crohn disease is associated with **NOD2** mutations.

NOD2 is an innate immune receptor (early immune sensor) that helps recognize bacterial cell wall components, so defective sensing can lead to abnormal gut immune responses.
