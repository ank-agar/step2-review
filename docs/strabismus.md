# Strabismus

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK11545/bin/space_perceptionf7.jpg)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/82/Esotropia-uncorrected.jpg)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/53/Diplopia.jpg)

Strabismus = misalignment of the eyes.

Basically, the two eyes are not pointing at the same target.

Common types:

* **Esotropia:** one eye turns inward toward the nose
* **Exotropia:** one eye turns outward away from the nose
* **Hypertropia:** one eye turns upward
* **Hypotropia:** one eye turns downward

---

### Core idea

Normally, both eyes look at the same object, and the brain fuses the two images into one 3D image.

In strabismus:

* one eye is aligned normally
* the other eye is deviated
* the brain receives two different images

So the patient can get:

* diplopia (double vision)
* poor depth perception
* amblyopia (decreased vision from abnormal visual development), especially in kids

---

### Why kids can get amblyopia

In a child, the brain is still learning how to use input from both eyes.

If one eye is constantly misaligned, the brain may suppress that eye's image to avoid double vision.

That suppression is useful short-term, but bad long-term:

* the brain stops using that eye well
* the visual pathway from that eye develops poorly
* vision becomes weaker in that eye

That is amblyopia, also called lazy eye.

---

### Why adults get double vision

Adults already have mature visual pathways.

So if strabismus suddenly develops, the brain usually cannot suppress the abnormal image as easily.

Result:

* two eyes send two different images
* brain cannot fuse them
* patient sees diplopia (double vision)

---

## One-line intuition

Strabismus is when the eyes are not aimed at the same thing, so the brain gets mismatched visual input.

---
