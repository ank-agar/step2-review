### Core idea

Hypersplenism = an overactive enlarged spleen that traps and/or destroys too many blood cells.

So you get low blood counts:

* anemia = low RBCs (red blood cells)
* leukopenia = low WBCs (white blood cells)
* thrombocytopenia = low platelets
* pancytopenia = low RBCs + WBCs + platelets

---

### What is the spleen normally doing?

The spleen is basically a blood filter.

It removes:

* old RBCs
* damaged RBCs
* abnormal cells
* some platelets and WBCs

Normally this is useful.

In hypersplenism, the spleen becomes too big and too aggressive, so it starts removing normal cells too.

---

### Why does an enlarged spleen cause low blood counts?

Two big mechanisms:

* sequestration = trapping/pooling cells inside the spleen
* destruction = macrophages (immune cleanup cells) eat the trapped cells

Think:

> big spleen = bigger blood-cell parking lot + more blood-cell cleanup

So fewer cells remain in the bloodstream.

---

### Classic test/lab pattern

Hypersplenism usually has:

* splenomegaly = enlarged spleen
* cytopenias = low blood cell counts
* normal or hypercellular bone marrow

Hypercellular bone marrow means the marrow is trying to compensate by making more cells.

That is important because the problem is not mainly that the marrow cannot produce cells.

The problem is:

> marrow makes cells, but the spleen traps/destroys them.

---

### Common causes

Anything that causes splenomegaly can lead to hypersplenism.

High-yield causes:

* portal hypertension = high pressure in the portal venous system, often from cirrhosis
* hemolytic anemias = disorders where RBCs are destroyed
* chronic infections
* leukemias/lymphomas = blood cancers
* storage diseases like Gaucher disease

Portal hypertension is especially HY because venous backup makes the spleen congested and enlarged.

---

## One-line intuition

Hypersplenism is when a big spleen acts like an overpowered blood filter, pulling too many RBCs, WBCs, and platelets out of circulation.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Hypersplenism causes cytopenias with a normal/hypercellular marrow.

That helps distinguish it from aplastic anemia, where the marrow is hypocellular, meaning it is not making enough cells in the first place.
