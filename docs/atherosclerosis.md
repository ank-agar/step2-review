### Core idea

Atherosclerosis means plaque buildup inside artery walls.

Break it down:

* athero- = fatty plaque
* sclerosis = hardening

So atherosclerosis is basically:

> fatty plaque builds up in arteries, making them narrow and stiff.

Plaque is made of cholesterol, inflammatory cells, dead cell debris, calcium, and fibrous tissue.

---

### How it starts

The key first step is endothelial injury.

Endothelium means the thin inner lining of a blood vessel.

Things that injure endothelium:

* hypertension (high blood pressure)
* smoking
* diabetes mellitus (high blood sugar disease)
* high LDL (low-density lipoprotein, the "bad cholesterol")

Once the endothelium is damaged, LDL can enter the vessel wall.

Then LDL gets oxidized, meaning chemically damaged.

Macrophages (cleanup immune cells) eat the oxidized LDL and become foam cells.

Foam cells are lipid-filled macrophages.

That early lesion is called a fatty streak.

---

### Why the artery narrows

The body tries to wall off the inflammation by forming a fibrous cap.

Fibrous cap means a tough collagen-rich covering over the lipid core.

Over time, the plaque bulges into the lumen.

Lumen means the open space where blood flows.

So:

* bigger plaque
* smaller lumen
* less blood flow
* less oxygen delivery downstream

That causes ischemia, meaning low blood flow causing low oxygen.

---

### Why plaque rupture is dangerous

The most dangerous event is plaque rupture.

If the fibrous cap breaks, the blood sees the inside of the plaque and treats it like a vessel injury.

That triggers:

* platelet adhesion
* clotting cascade activation
* thrombus formation

Thrombus means blood clot.

If the clot blocks the artery:

* coronary artery blockage = myocardial infarction, or MI (heart attack)
* carotid/cerebral artery blockage = stroke
* leg artery blockage = peripheral artery disease, or PAD (poor blood flow to limbs)

---

### Stable vs unstable plaque

Stable plaques usually have:

* thick fibrous cap
* smaller lipid core
* more gradual narrowing

These tend to cause chronic ischemia, like stable angina (chest pain with exertion).

Unstable plaques usually have:

* thin fibrous cap
* large lipid core
* lots of inflammation

These are more likely to rupture and suddenly clot.

That is why a smaller-looking plaque can still cause a huge acute event if it ruptures.

---

## One-line intuition

Atherosclerosis is cholesterol-driven inflammation in artery walls that slowly narrows vessels, but becomes acutely dangerous when a plaque ruptures and forms a clot.

---

## Pearl 🔥

In atherosclerosis, macrophages eat oxidized LDL and turn into foam cells.

Foam cells are the key cell in the fatty streak, which is the earliest visible lesion of atherosclerosis.
