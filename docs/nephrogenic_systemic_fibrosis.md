## Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis

Nephrogenic systemic fibrosis (NSF) is a rare fibrosing disease in patients with severe kidney dysfunction, classically after exposure to gadolinium-based contrast agents (GBCAs) used for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Break the name apart:

* nephrogenic = related to the kidney
* systemic = affects multiple body areas
* fibrosis = excess collagen/scar-like tissue

So the core idea is:

> bad kidneys cannot clear gadolinium well, so gadolinium hangs around and can trigger widespread fibrosis.

### What happens

Patients develop thick, tight, hard skin, especially on the:

* arms
* legs
* trunk

The skin can feel woody and can cause:

* itching or burning pain
* decreased joint movement
* contractures (fixed shortening/tightening around joints)

Face is usually spared, which helps distinguish it from some scleroderma-like disorders.

### Why kidney failure matters

Normally, gadolinium is cleared by the kidneys.

In severe renal failure:

* gadolinium stays in the body longer
* it may dissociate from its chelator (carrier molecule)
* free gadolinium can deposit in tissues
* macrophages (immune cleanup cells) get activated
* fibrocytes/fibroblasts make excess collagen

That excess collagen causes the tight, stiff, scar-like skin.

### Classic risk setup

Think:

* severe chronic kidney disease (CKD; chronic kidney disease)
* end-stage renal disease (ESRD; end-stage renal disease)
* dialysis patient
* recent gadolinium contrast MRI

Highest risk is with older, less stable linear gadolinium agents.

### One-line intuition

NSF is basically gadolinium-triggered "internal scarring" in a patient whose kidneys cannot clear the gadolinium.

### HY pearl

Before giving gadolinium contrast, always think about glomerular filtration rate (GFR; kidney filtration rate): NSF risk is highest when GFR is severely reduced, especially below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2.
