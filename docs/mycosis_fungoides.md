### Core idea

Mycosis fungoides is the most common type of cutaneous T-cell lymphoma (CTCL = T-cell lymphoma involving the skin).

It is not a fungal infection.

The name sounds fungal because advanced tumors can look mushroom-like, but the actual problem is:

* malignant CD4+ T cells (cancerous helper T cells)
* accumulating in the skin
* causing chronic rash-like lesions

---

### Classic progression

Think:

* patch
* plaque
* tumor

Patch = flat, scaly rash-like area  
Plaque = raised, thickened lesion  
Tumor = deeper nodular mass

This is why it often gets mistaken for eczema or psoriasis early on.

---

### Why the skin findings happen

The malignant T cells are epidermotropic (they like moving into the epidermis, the outer skin layer).

So they infiltrate skin and cause:

* itchy patches
* scaly plaques
* thick tumors in later disease

On biopsy, you can see Pautrier microabscesses (collections of malignant T cells in the epidermis).

The malignant cells can have cerebriform nuclei (brain-like folded nuclei).

---

### Important association

If the malignant T cells spread into the blood, that is called Sezary syndrome.

Sezary syndrome classically causes:

* diffuse erythroderma (widespread red skin)
* lymphadenopathy (enlarged lymph nodes)
* malignant T cells in blood called Sezary cells

---

## One-line intuition

Mycosis fungoides is basically a slow skin-homing CD4+ T-cell cancer that starts like eczema, then can progress to plaques and tumors.
