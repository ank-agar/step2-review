### Core idea

ABO incompatibility in a newborn means the mom and baby have incompatible ABO blood types (A, B, AB, or O blood groups), so mom's antibodies attack the baby's RBCs (red blood cells).

Classic setup:

* mom = type O
* baby = type A, B, or AB

Type O moms can have anti-A and anti-B IgG antibodies (immunoglobulin G antibodies), and IgG can cross the placenta.

---

### What happens

Mom's IgG antibodies cross the placenta and bind the baby's RBCs.

Then:

* antibody-coated RBCs get destroyed = hemolysis (RBC breakdown)
* hemolysis releases heme
* heme becomes bilirubin
* bilirubin builds up = jaundice (yellow skin/eyes)

So the main newborn finding is:

* early jaundice, often within the first 24 hours

---

### Why this causes jaundice

RBC breakdown makes lots of unconjugated bilirubin (bilirubin not yet processed by the liver).

Newborn livers are immature, so they cannot conjugate (process for excretion) bilirubin fast enough.

So:

> more RBC destruction + immature liver = bilirubin rises fast.

---

### Labs

Common findings:

* positive direct Coombs test, also called DAT (direct antiglobulin test)
* increased unconjugated bilirubin
* possible mild anemia (low RBC/hemoglobin level)
* increased reticulocytes (young RBCs), because bone marrow tries to replace destroyed RBCs

Direct Coombs test means:

> antibodies are already stuck to the baby's RBCs.

---

### Why ABO disease is usually milder than Rh disease

Rh disease (incompatibility involving the Rh blood group, especially D antigen) can be severe.

ABO incompatibility is usually milder because:

* fetal RBCs express less A/B antigen
* A/B antigens are also on other tissues, so some antibodies get "soaked up" before destroying RBCs

---

## One-line intuition

Mom's type O IgG antibodies cross the placenta, tag the baby's A/B RBCs for destruction, and the RBC breakdown causes early newborn jaundice.

---

## Pearl 🔥

ABO incompatibility can affect the first pregnancy because anti-A and anti-B antibodies can already exist naturally, unlike classic Rh disease, which usually needs prior sensitization.
