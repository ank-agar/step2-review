## Pulmonary contusion

![Image](https://www.netterimages.com/images/vpv/000/000/035/35790-0550x0475.jpg)

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK558914/bin/pul__contusion.jpg)

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK621650/bin/626651_1_En_10_Fig3_HTML.gif)

Pulmonary contusion = a **bruise of the lung** after trauma.

More specifically:

* pulmonary = lung
* contusion = bruise

So it means the lung tissue gets injured, causing:

* bleeding into alveoli (tiny air sacs)
* edema (fluid swelling)
* impaired gas exchange

---

### Core idea

The lung is not cut open.

Instead, blunt trauma damages the alveolar-capillary membrane, which is the thin barrier between:

* alveoli (air spaces)
* capillaries (tiny blood vessels)

Blood and fluid leak into the alveoli, so oxygen cannot diffuse well into blood.

That causes:

* dyspnea (shortness of breath)
* hypoxemia (low blood oxygen)
* tachypnea (fast breathing)
* crackles

---

### Why the imaging looks white

Normally, lungs look dark on chest X-ray because they are full of air.

In pulmonary contusion, the alveoli fill with blood/fluid instead of air.

Fluid/blood is more dense than air, so it appears as patchy white opacity on chest X-ray or CT (computed tomography).

---

### Common setting

Usually after blunt chest trauma, especially:

* motor vehicle collision
* rib fractures
* fall
* crush injury

Important: symptoms and X-ray findings can worsen over the first 24-48 hours because edema and inflammation build up after the initial injury.

---

## One-line intuition

Pulmonary contusion is a lung bruise where trauma makes alveoli fill with blood/fluid, so the patient cannot oxygenate well.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Pulmonary contusion can cause hypoxemia without pneumothorax (air in the pleural space) because the problem is **inside the lung tissue/alveoli**, not necessarily air outside the lung.
