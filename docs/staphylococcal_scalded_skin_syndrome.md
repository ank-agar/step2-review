### Core idea

Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome is a toxin-mediated skin disease caused by **Staphylococcus aureus**.

The bacteria make **exfoliative toxin** (a toxin that makes skin layers peel apart).

This toxin breaks **desmoglein-1** (a protein that helps epidermal skin cells stick together).

So the superficial epidermis separates and peels off, making the skin look “scalded.”

---

### Why the skin peels

Think:

* desmoglein-1 = glue between superficial skin cells
* exfoliative toxin = scissors cutting the glue
* cut glue = skin cells separate
* separated superficial epidermis = flaccid blisters + peeling

The split happens high up in the epidermis, around the **stratum granulosum** (granular layer of the epidermis).

---

### Classic findings

Usually in infants/young children:

* fever
* irritability
* diffuse tender redness
* flaccid bullae (loose blisters)
* skin sloughing/peeling
* positive Nikolsky sign (skin shears off with gentle pressure)

Important:

* mucous membranes are usually spared

Why?

Mucosa has more **desmoglein-3** (another skin-cell adhesion protein), which can compensate when desmoglein-1 is damaged.

---

### Key distinction

SSSS (staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome) is toxin spread through blood.

The blister fluid is usually sterile because the bacteria are often sitting somewhere else, like:

* nose/throat
* conjunctiva (eye lining)
* umbilicus
* wound

So the toxin travels, not necessarily the bacteria.

---

## One-line intuition

Staph aureus makes a toxin that cuts the superficial skin “glue,” so the top layer of skin lifts off like a scald.

---

## Pearl 🔥

SSSS classically has **no mucosal involvement**, which helps separate it from **SJS/TEN** (Stevens-Johnson syndrome/toxic epidermal necrolysis), where mucosal involvement is common and severe.
