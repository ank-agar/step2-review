### Core idea

A lung abscess is a pus-filled cavity inside the lung parenchyma (functional lung tissue).

Think:

* pneumonia/infection kills lung tissue
* dead tissue liquefies into pus
* a cavity forms
* if it connects to an airway, air enters the cavity

That gives the classic imaging finding:

* air on top
* fluid/pus on bottom
* air-fluid level

---

### Why it happens

Most Step 1 style cause:

* aspiration of oral contents

Aspiration means inhaling material from the mouth or stomach into the lungs.

This matters because the mouth has anaerobes (bacteria that grow without oxygen). If someone aspirates oral bacteria into dependent lung regions, those bacteria can cause necrotizing infection.

Necrotizing means tissue death.

So the chain is:

> aspiration -> anaerobic infection -> tissue necrosis -> pus cavity -> lung abscess

---

### Classic risk factors

Anything that increases aspiration risk:

* alcoholism
* seizure
* stroke
* anesthesia/sedation
* poor dentition
* altered consciousness

Poor dentition matters because bad teeth/gums increase the bacterial load in the mouth.

---

### Symptoms

Usually more gradual than typical pneumonia:

* fever
* cough
* night sweats
* weight loss
* foul-smelling sputum

Foul-smelling sputum is a big clue for anaerobic infection.

---

### Imaging clue

Chest imaging often shows a thick-walled cavity with an air-fluid level.

Why air-fluid level?

* pus/fluid sinks to the bottom
* air rises to the top
* the flat line between them is the air-fluid level

---

## One-line intuition

A lung abscess is basically a pocket of pus in the lung, usually from aspirated mouth anaerobes destroying lung tissue.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Aspiration lung abscesses usually localize to dependent lung segments:

* right lower lobe if upright
* posterior upper lobes or superior lower lobes if lying down

Right side is common because the right main bronchus is wider, shorter, and more vertical.
