### Core idea

Hyperkalemia = high potassium in the blood.

Usually this means serum K+ (potassium ion) is > 5.0 mEq/L.

The big danger is the heart, because potassium controls resting membrane potential (the baseline electrical voltage across a cell membrane).

---

### Why high K+ affects the heart

Normally:

* K+ is high inside cells
* K+ is low outside cells
* K+ wants to leave the cell, helping keep the inside negative

In hyperkalemia:

* outside K+ is higher than normal
* the K+ gradient is smaller
* less K+ leaves the cell
* the cell becomes less negative/depolarized

At first, this makes repolarization faster, causing:

* tall peaked T waves on ECG (electrocardiogram)

But if K+ gets very high, sodium channels stay inactivated, so conduction slows, causing:

* PR prolongation
* flattened/absent P waves
* widened QRS
* sine-wave pattern
* ventricular fibrillation/asystole

---

### Common causes

Think: too much K+ in blood because kidneys cannot remove it, cells release it, or drugs block excretion.

* renal failure
* ACE inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors)
* ARBs (angiotensin receptor blockers)
* spironolactone/eplerenone
* trimethoprim
* heparin
* acidosis
* rhabdomyolysis (skeletal muscle breakdown)
* tumor lysis syndrome (massive cancer cell breakdown after treatment)

---

### Treatment intuition

There are 3 different goals:

* Stabilize the heart: IV calcium
* Shift K+ into cells: insulin + glucose, beta-agonists, bicarbonate if acidotic
* Remove K+ from body: loop diuretics, potassium binders, dialysis

Calcium does not lower K+.

It just protects the cardiac membrane from the high K+.

---

## One-line intuition

Hyperkalemia makes cardiac cells electrically unstable: early peaked T waves, then slow conduction, wide QRS, and possible arrest.

---

## Pearl 🔥

In DKA (diabetic ketoacidosis), serum K+ may be high because acidosis/insulin deficiency shifts K+ out of cells, but total body K+ is usually low from urinary losses.
