## Osteosarcoma

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK549764/bin/codman__triangle.jpg)

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK549764/bin/osteosarcoma.jpg)

Osteosarcoma is a malignant bone tumor where cancer cells make osteoid (immature bone matrix).

Think:

* osteo = bone
* sarcoma = malignant tumor from mesenchymal tissue (connective tissue, bone, cartilage, muscle, fat)

So osteosarcoma = malignant bone-forming tumor.

---

### Classic patient

Usually a teenager with:

* bone pain
* swelling
* lesion near the knee

Most common sites:

* distal femur
* proximal tibia

These are metaphyseal regions (the flared part of a long bone near the growth plate).

---

### Why it happens near the knee

The metaphysis is a very active bone-growth area in kids/adolescents.

More growth = more cell division.

More cell division = more chances for mutations.

That is why osteosarcoma likes rapidly growing long bones.

---

### Classic imaging

On X-ray (radiograph), look for:

* sunburst pattern
* Codman triangle

Sunburst happens because the tumor grows aggressively and pushes through bone, causing spicules (thin spikes) of new bone to radiate outward.

Codman triangle happens when the tumor lifts the periosteum (outer bone covering), and new reactive bone forms at the edge.

---

### Histology idea

The key microscopic finding is:

* malignant cells producing osteoid

That is the defining feature.

Not just "bone tumor" - the malignant tumor cells themselves are making immature bone matrix.

---

### Associations

Risk is increased with:

* RB mutation (retinoblastoma gene mutation)
* Li-Fraumeni syndrome from TP53 mutation (tumor suppressor gene mutation)
* Paget disease of bone in older adults
* prior radiation exposure

---

## One-line intuition

Osteosarcoma is an aggressive bone cancer of adolescents that grows near the knee and makes messy immature bone.

---

## HY pearl

Osteosarcoma is classically metaphyseal, while Ewing sarcoma is classically diaphyseal (shaft of long bone) with onion-skin periosteal reaction.
