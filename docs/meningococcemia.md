### Core idea

Meningococcemia means **Neisseria meningitidis in the blood**.

Break the word apart:

* meningo- = meningococcus / Neisseria meningitidis
* -coccemia = cocci bacteria in the blood

So:

> meningococcemia = bloodstream infection by meningococcus.

This is a medical emergency because it can rapidly cause:

* sepsis
* shock
* disseminated intravascular coagulation, or DIC (widespread clotting that uses up platelets and clotting factors)
* petechiae/purpura (small or larger purple skin bleeds)
* adrenal hemorrhage in severe cases

---

### Why the rash happens

Neisseria meningitidis is a **gram-negative diplococcus**.

* gram-negative = has lipooligosaccharide/endotoxin (toxin-like outer membrane molecule)
* diplococcus = round bacteria in pairs

The endotoxin triggers a huge inflammatory response:

* cytokines (immune signaling proteins) go up
* blood vessels get damaged and leaky
* clotting gets activated inside small vessels
* platelets and clotting factors get used up

That gives:

* **petechiae** = tiny pinpoint skin bleeds
* **purpura** = larger purple skin bleeds
* **purpura fulminans** = severe purpura with skin necrosis (tissue death)

So the rash is not just "skin infection."

It is a clue that the bacteria are causing **vascular injury + DIC**.

---

### Classic presentation

Think:

* fever
* toxic appearance
* hypotension (low blood pressure)
* rapidly spreading petechial or purpuric rash
* possible meningitis symptoms: headache, neck stiffness, photophobia (light sensitivity)

Important:

Meningococcemia can happen **with or without meningitis**.

Meningitis means infection/inflammation of the meninges (brain and spinal cord coverings).

Meningococcemia means the organism is mainly in the **bloodstream**.

---

### Why it spreads fast

Neisseria meningitidis has a capsule.

Capsule = slippery outer coat that helps bacteria avoid phagocytosis (immune cell eating).

That lets it survive in blood and cause invasive disease.

It spreads through respiratory droplets, usually from close contact, because it can colonize the nasopharynx (back of the nose/throat).

---

### Big Step 1 associations

High risk with:

* terminal complement deficiency, especially C5-C9
* asplenia (no spleen or poorly functioning spleen)
* college dorms/military barracks
* complement inhibitor drugs like eculizumab

Why C5-C9 matters:

C5-C9 forms the membrane attack complex, or MAC (immune pore that punches holes in bacteria).

Neisseria species are especially vulnerable to MAC.

So low terminal complement = recurrent Neisseria infections.

---

### Treatment/prevention idea

Suspected meningococcemia needs immediate antibiotics, classically ceftriaxone.

Close contacts need prophylaxis (preventive antibiotics), such as rifampin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone depending on the situation.

Vaccines:

* MenACWY covers serogroups A, C, W, Y
* MenB covers serogroup B

Serogroup = subtype based on the bacterial capsule.

---

## One-line intuition

Meningococcemia is **meningococcus in the blood**, where its endotoxin damages vessels, triggers DIC, and causes the classic rapidly spreading petechial/purpuric rash.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Recurrent Neisseria infections should make you think **terminal complement deficiency C5-C9**, because C5-C9 forms the MAC (membrane attack complex) that kills Neisseria.
