### Core idea

Spinal cord transection = the spinal cord is cut/severed across its width.

So signals cannot pass between:

* brain
* body below the lesion

Think:

> the main cable between brain and body is cut.

---

### What gets lost below the lesion

Because the cord carries both directions of information:

* **descending motor tracts** = brain commands going down to muscles
* **ascending sensory tracts** = body sensation going up to brain

A complete transection causes:

* motor paralysis below the lesion
* sensory loss below the lesion
* autonomic dysfunction below the lesion

Autonomic dysfunction means problems with involuntary functions like blood pressure, sweating, bladder control, and bowel control.

---

### Why the level matters

The higher the lesion, the more body is disconnected.

* cervical lesion = can affect arms + legs
* thoracic lesion = usually affects legs/trunk more than arms
* lesion above C3-C5 can impair breathing

C3-C5 is important because the phrenic nerve comes from C3-C5 and controls the diaphragm.

---

### Acute finding

Right after severe spinal cord injury, you can get **spinal shock**.

Spinal shock = temporary loss of reflexes, tone, and autonomic function below the lesion.

So early on:

* flaccid paralysis
* absent reflexes
* loss of sensation

Later, reflexes can return and become exaggerated because the brain is no longer inhibiting spinal reflex arcs.

---

## One-line intuition

Spinal cord transection cuts the main communication highway, so everything below the cut loses brain input and cannot send sensation back up.

---

## Pearl 🔥

Lesions above T6 can cause **autonomic dysreflexia**: a dangerous sympathetic reflex causing severe hypertension, often triggered by bladder or bowel distension below the injury.
