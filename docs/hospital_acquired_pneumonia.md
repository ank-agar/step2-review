## Hospital-acquired pneumonia

![Image](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/images/ency/fullsize/17276.jpg)

![Image](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507711/bin/00108310_cw_image_wi_f1a58a6fbd23d0a3c4be9236cf5c38f8.jpg)

### Core idea

Hospital-acquired pneumonia is pneumonia that develops **48 hours or more after hospital admission** and was not incubating when the patient arrived.

Pneumonia = infection/inflammation of lung alveoli (tiny air sacs where gas exchange happens).

So basically:

> patient comes to hospital for something else, then develops a lung infection from hospital-associated organisms.

---

### Why it is different from community-acquired pneumonia

Hospital bugs are often more dangerous because they are more likely to be:

* gram-negative rods
* Staphylococcus aureus
* resistant to antibiotics

Important organisms:

* Pseudomonas aeruginosa
* MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus)
* Klebsiella
* Enterobacter
* Escherichia coli
* Acinetobacter

That is why treatment usually needs broader antibiotic coverage than regular community-acquired pneumonia.

---

### Why hospitalized patients get it

Hospitalized patients often have weaker lung defenses:

* lying in bed decreases clearance of secretions
* altered mental status increases aspiration risk
* intubation bypasses normal airway defenses
* prior antibiotics select for resistant bacteria
* severe illness weakens immune response

Aspiration means inhaling oral or gastric contents into the airway.

---

### Ventilator-associated pneumonia

Ventilator-associated pneumonia is a subtype of hospital-acquired pneumonia that occurs **48 hours or more after intubation**.

Intubation = placing a breathing tube into the trachea (windpipe).

Why the tube increases risk:

* it bypasses the mouth/nose filtering system
* it blocks normal coughing
* bacteria can form biofilm on the tube
* contaminated secretions can leak around the cuff into the lungs

Biofilm = a sticky bacterial layer that protects bacteria from antibiotics and immune cells.

---

### Clinical picture

Think pneumonia in a hospitalized patient:

* fever
* cough
* purulent sputum
* hypoxia (low oxygen)
* increased white blood cells
* new infiltrate on chest x-ray

Infiltrate = abnormal opacity on imaging, often from fluid, pus, or inflammatory cells filling alveoli.

---

## One-line intuition

Hospital-acquired pneumonia is pneumonia from hospital-associated, often resistant bacteria in a patient whose normal lung defenses are impaired.

---

## Pearl 🔥

For Step 1, remember: **HAP (hospital-acquired pneumonia) = 48+ hours after admission**, and **VAP (ventilator-associated pneumonia) = 48+ hours after intubation**.
