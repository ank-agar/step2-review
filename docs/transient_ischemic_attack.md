### Core idea

A transient ischemic attack, or **TIA (transient ischemic attack)**, is a temporary episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by brief ischemia (low blood flow) to part of the brain, spinal cord, or retina (light-sensing layer of the eye).

Think:

* stroke-like symptoms
* caused by temporary blocked blood flow
* symptoms go away
* no permanent infarction (tissue death from loss of blood supply)

---

### Why the symptoms happen

The brain depends on constant blood flow for oxygen/glucose.

If a small embolus (traveling clot) or thrombus (clot formed in place) briefly blocks an artery:

* that brain area loses function
* symptoms appear suddenly
* then the clot dissolves or moves
* blood flow returns
* symptoms resolve

So a TIA is basically a **temporary ischemic stroke without permanent brain tissue death**.

---

### Classic symptoms

Same symptoms as a stroke, but transient (temporary):

* unilateral weakness/numbness = one-sided face/arm/leg symptoms
* aphasia (language problem, like trouble speaking or understanding)
* dysarthria (slurred speech due to motor speech problem)
* vision loss
* dizziness/ataxia (poor coordination)

Retinal TIA can cause **amaurosis fugax (temporary painless monocular vision loss)**.

---

### TIA vs stroke

* **TIA:** temporary ischemia, symptoms resolve, no infarction
* **Ischemic stroke:** prolonged ischemia causing infarction

Important: clinically, you cannot safely assume it is "just a TIA" while symptoms are happening.

Stroke symptoms = emergency evaluation.

---

## One-line intuition

A TIA is a clot briefly blocking brain blood flow, causing stroke symptoms that disappear before permanent tissue death occurs.

---

## Pearl 🔥

TIA is a major warning sign for future ischemic stroke, commonly from carotid atherosclerosis (cholesterol plaque buildup in the carotid artery) or atrial fibrillation (irregular atrial rhythm causing emboli).
