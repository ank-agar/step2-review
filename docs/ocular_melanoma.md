## Ocular melanoma

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1e/Schematic_diagram_of_the_human_eye_en.svg)

![Image](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/6b/Iris_melanoma.jpg)

### Core idea

Ocular melanoma is melanoma inside or around the eye.

Melanoma = cancer of melanocytes (pigment-producing cells).

Most ocular melanomas are **uveal melanomas**.

Uvea = the pigmented middle layer of the eye, including:

* iris
* ciliary body
* choroid

So the most common intuition is:

> pigment-cell cancer growing in the vascular pigmented layer of the eye.

---

### Why it happens in the eye

The eye has melanocytes because pigment helps absorb extra light.

If those melanocytes become malignant (cancerous), they can form a dark tumor in places like the:

* iris = colored part of the eye
* choroid = vascular layer behind the retina
* ciliary body = helps make aqueous humor (fluid in the front of the eye) and control lens shape

---

### Symptoms

Can be asymptomatic (no symptoms), especially early.

When symptomatic, it can cause:

* blurry vision
* floaters
* flashes of light
* visual field loss
* visible dark spot on the iris
* retinal detachment if the tumor lifts the retina

Retinal detachment happens because a growing choroidal tumor can physically push the retina away from the back wall of the eye.

---

### High-yield spread pattern

Ocular melanoma classically metastasizes (spreads) to the **liver**.

Why?

The uveal tract has lots of blood vessels and limited lymphatic drainage, so spread is mainly hematogenous (through blood), not lymphatic.

---

## One-line intuition

Ocular melanoma is a pigment-cell cancer of the eye, usually from the uvea, that is especially high-yield for blood spread to the liver.

---

## Pearl

Uveal melanoma is genetically different from cutaneous melanoma (skin melanoma): it is classically associated with **GNAQ/GNA11** mutations, while skin melanoma is more associated with **BRAF** mutations.
